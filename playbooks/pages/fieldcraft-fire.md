## Lighting a fire here

A fire lights in Britain in any month, in most weather, from what grows within a few hundred metres of almost anywhere: birch bark, gorse, dead bracken and dead standing wood are on nearly every heath, hedge and hillside. What decides it is preparation, not the climate. A fire fails when the tinder is not fine enough, the kindling is too thick, the lay is too loose to hold heat, or the first thicker wood is green or soaked from lying on the ground. Get those four right and the weather is a detail.

## Tinder that grows here

- **Birch bark**: the papery outer bark of silver and downy birch burns even when wet because of its oils. Take loose curls, never ring the tree ([Silver birch](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Betula_pendula)).
- **Gorse**: the dead spiny twigs inside a gorse bush are dry in any weather and go up like paper ([Gorse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ulex)).
- **Dead bracken**, dry brown and standing, crumbled fine; dead grass heads; the fluffy seed heads of thistles, rosebay willowherb and reedmace.
- **Honeysuckle and old man's beard**: their shredded bark makes a fine nest for a spark.
- **Fatwood**: the resin-soaked heart of a pine stump or a dead lower branch, shaved thin, burns hot and wet.
- **From the house**: cotton wool smeared with petroleum jelly, a tampon, tumble-dryer lint, wax paper, a candle stub, a strip of bicycle inner tube for the worst weather. Carry two lighters and a ferro rod as well as matches.

## Wood

Take **dead standing wood**: dead branches still on trees, or dead saplings that snap cleanly. Wood lying on the ground is wet inside after rain; if it is all there is, split it, because the inside of a thick piece is dry when the outside is soaked. Feather sticks, a stick shaved into a curl of thin feathers with a knife, turn one damp branch into kindling that lights from a spark ([Feather stick](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Feather_stick)). Grade the wood before you strike anything: a fistful of match-thin twigs, an armful of pencil thickness, then finger and wrist thickness, all within reach. Ash, beech, oak, hawthorn and birch burn well; willow, poplar and alder poorly; green wood not at all; treated or painted timber never, because of the fumes.

## Building it

Build on a base of sticks or bark, and out of the wind or behind a reflector of logs or stones. Put the tinder bundle down, lean the thinnest kindling over it in a small tepee or lean-to against a thicker stick, and keep the next two grades ready. Light the tinder at the bottom, upwind, and feed with thin sticks faster than you think, then slow down. Do not add anything thick until the fire has a bed of embers; do not crowd it; do not blow until it needs air. A fire lay called the **long fire**, two logs parallel with the fire between, holds heat, cooks and dries kit at once; a criss-cross of split wood makes a bed of cooking embers ([Camping and Woodcraft, the fire chapters](doc:kephart-camping-and-woodcraft)).

## In rain

The same fire, with more care at the start. Work under the tarp edge or a large sheet of bark, split everything down to the dry core, use twice the tinder and twice the thin kindling you think you need, and keep the reserve wood inside your jacket while the fire takes. Once it has embers it will burn damp wood laid on the sides to dry, and a rain-fed fire is no different from any other.

## Keeping it and using it

Bank it for the night with larger logs and ash over the embers; it will relight in the morning from a puff of air and a handful of tinder. Carry fire in a tin of embers packed in dry moss if you move. Cook on embers, not flames. Dry clothes on a rack of sticks at arm's length, never over the flames. Sleep with the fire outside the shelter, never inside a closed one: carbon monoxide kills people in tents and bothies ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).

## Without a lighter

A ferro rod and a knife spine throw sparks into a tinder nest in any weather and work wet. A magnifying glass or a spectacle lens works on a sunny day. Flint and steel needs char cloth, which you can make in a tin in the fire. The bow drill works in Britain with dry lime, willow, sycamore or ivy for the spindle and board; practise it when the wood is dry so it is there when you need it ([Bow drill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bow_drill)).

## Putting it out

Drown it, stir it, drown it again, feel it cold with the back of the hand. Scatter the cold ash, replace the turf, leave no ring of stones. A peat fire can smoulder underground for days and re-emerge metres away, which is why peat is the one ground you never build on.

## The law and peat

In normal times most uplands, heaths, commons and access land forbid open fires, and a moorland fire in a dry spring can burn for a week and destroy a peat bog that took thousands of years to form. The Scottish access code allows small fires, away from peat, with care and leaving no trace ([Scottish Outdoor Access Code](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scottish_Outdoor_Access_Code)). When society has stopped working the law is no longer the point, but the peat still is: never light a fire on peat, heather or dry grass in a drought, and never leave one.

## Stoves, for when a fire is not on

A stove is for the places and times a fire is not: a fire ban, a tent porch in a downpour, a quick brew on the move, or nothing to burn. It fails for reasons you can plan for.

- **Gas**: the screw-thread canisters sold in outdoor shops hold a butane, isobutane and propane mix ([EN 417](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/EN_417)). Butane stops boiling off at about −1 °C ([Butane](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Butane)) and isobutane at about −12 °C ([Isobutane](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Isobutane)), so on a frosty morning a canister that is mostly butane gives a weak yellow flame or nothing; canister stoves are not recommended in freezing temperatures at all ([TC 3-97.61, p. 82](doc:tc-3-97-61-military-mountaineering#page=82)). Keep the canister warm in a pocket or the sleeping bag before use, stand it in a shallow dish of water (never heat it), and choose a propane-heavy mix for winter. Change canisters away from any flame, and check the seal by listening.
- **Meths (methylated spirit)**: a Trangia-type spirit burner has no moving parts, lights in any weather, burns quietly and slowly, and the fuel is sold in hardware shops ([Alcohol burner](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Alcohol_burner)). The flame is invisible in daylight: never refill a burner that might still be alight, and keep the bottle well away.
- **Liquid fuel** (petrol, paraffin, diesel): multi-fuel stoves are heavier and need priming and cleaning, but they work in cold and burn whatever you can find ([TC 3-97.61, p. 82](doc:tc-3-97-61-military-mountaineering#page=82)).
- **How much**: consumption depends on the stove, the wind, the water temperature and how often you cook. Measure your own: weigh a canister or a fuel bottle before and after a normal day, plan on that figure, and carry a spare. A windshield and a lid cut the fuel used; unprotected in wind, a stove wastes most of its heat ([TC 3-97.61, p. 82](doc:tc-3-97-61-military-mountaineering#page=82)).
- **Storage**: fuel lives outside the sleeping space, upright, cool and out of the sun, away from the cooking area and any flame; gas canisters and fuel bottles are never left in a hot car or a tent porch in sun. Any stove used inside a closed tent, a bothy or a car makes carbon monoxide ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).
