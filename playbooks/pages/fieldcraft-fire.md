## Before you strike a match

Most of the British uplands, heaths, commons and access land forbid open fires, and a moorland fire in a dry April can burn for a week and destroy a peat bog that took eight thousand years to form. In normal times, cook on a stove: gas, meths or a wood-burning twig stove that lifts the fire off the ground. The Scottish access code allows small fires, away from peat, with care and leaving no trace ([Scottish Outdoor Access Code](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scottish_Outdoor_Access_Code)). When society has stopped working, the law is no longer the point, but the peat still is: never light a fire on peat, heather or dry grass in a drought, and never leave one.

## Why British fires fail

Everything is damp. Wood lying on the ground here is wet through, even in summer. The fire fails because the tinder is damp, the kindling is too thick, the fire lay is too loose to hold heat, or the wood on top is green or soaked. Fix those four things and a fire lights in the rain.

## Tinder you can find here

- **Birch bark**: the papery outer bark of silver and downy birch, which grow on almost every heath and hillside, burns even when wet because of its oils. Take loose curls, never ring the tree ([Silver birch](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Betula_pendula)).
- **Dead bracken**, dry brown and standing, crumbled fine; dead grass heads; the fluffy seed heads of thistles, rosebay willowherb and reedmace.
- **Gorse**: the dead spiny twigs inside a gorse bush are dry in any weather and go up like paper ([Gorse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ulex)).
- **Honeysuckle and old man's beard**: their shredded bark makes a fine nest for a spark.
- **Fatwood**: the resin-soaked heart of a pine stump or a dead lower branch, shaved thin, burns hot and wet.
- **From the house**: cotton wool smeared with petroleum jelly, a tampon, tumble-dryer lint, wax paper, a candle stub, a strip of bicycle inner tube for the worst weather. Carry two lighters and a ferro rod as well as matches.

## Wood

Never take wood from the ground. Take **dead standing wood**: dead branches still on trees, or dead saplings that snap cleanly. Split any thicker piece: the inside is dry when the outside is soaked. Feather sticks, a dry stick shaved into a curl of thin feathers with a knife, turn one damp branch into kindling that lights from a spark ([Feather stick](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Feather_stick)). Grade the wood before you strike anything: a fistful of match-thin twigs, an armful of pencil thickness, then finger and wrist thickness, all within reach. Ash, beech, oak, hawthorn and birch burn well; willow, poplar and alder poorly; green wood not at all; treated or painted timber never, because of the fumes.

## Building it

Build on a base of sticks or bark, not on wet ground, and out of the wind or behind a reflector of logs or stones. Put the tinder bundle down, lean the thinnest kindling over it in a small tepee or lean-to against a thicker stick, and keep the next two grades ready. Light the tinder at the bottom, upwind, and feed with thin sticks faster than you think, then slow down. Do not add anything thick until the fire has a bed of embers; do not crowd it; do not blow until it needs air. In rain, work under the tarp edge or a large sheet of bark, and keep the reserve wood in your jacket. A fire lay called the **long fire**, two logs parallel with the fire between, holds heat, cooks and dries kit at once; a criss-cross of split wood makes a bed of cooking embers.

## Keeping it and using it

Once established, a fire will burn damp wood laid on the sides to dry. Bank it for the night with larger logs and ash over the embers; it will relight in the morning from a puff of air and a handful of tinder. Carry fire in a tin of embers packed in dry moss if you move. Cook on embers, not flames. Dry clothes on a rack of sticks at arm's length, never over the flames. Sleep with the fire outside the shelter, never inside a closed one: carbon monoxide kills people in tents and bothies every winter ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).

## Without a lighter

A ferro rod and a knife spine throw sparks into a tinder nest in any weather and work wet. A magnifying glass or a spectacle lens works on a sunny day. Flint and steel needs char cloth, which you can make in a tin in the fire. The bow drill, the friction method, works in Britain with dry lime, willow, sycamore or ivy for the spindle and board, but it is a skill for practice in the dry, not a plan for the wet ([Bow drill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bow_drill)). Keep the tin of matches dry and the practice regular.

## Putting it out

Drown it, stir it, drown it again, feel it cold with the back of the hand. Scatter the cold ash, replace the turf, leave no ring of stones. A peat fire can smoulder underground for days and re-emerge metres away, which is why peat is the one ground you never build on.
