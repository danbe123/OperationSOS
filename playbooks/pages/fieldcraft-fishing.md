## The law in one line

In England and Wales anyone over 12 needs a rod licence to fish for salmon, trout, freshwater fish, smelt or eels, on top of the water owner's permission; sea fishing from the shore needs neither; close seasons, Scotland and Northern Ireland are in [foraging, fishing and hunting law](page:foraging-law) ([Fishing licence](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Fishing_license)). Set lines and nets are illegal on most inland waters ([Trotline](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Trotline)): the methods below beyond rod or hand line are for when the law no longer runs and the choice is between them and hunger.

## Where the fish are

- **Coarse fish** are what fill British canals, lakes, ponds and slow rivers: carp, bream, roach, chub, dace, perch, pike and eel. They take maggot, worm, bread, cheese, sweetcorn and luncheon meat, and coarse fishing is the technique of choice for still and slow water; trout and salmon are the game fish of the faster, colder rivers ([Coarse fishing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Coarse_fishing)).
- **Sea fish** from the shore cost no licence: mackerel come inshore in shoals in summer and take almost anything shiny ([Atlantic mackerel](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Atlantic_mackerel)); flounder lie on sandy estuary bottoms and take worm ([Flounder](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Flounder)); crabs and prawns come to bait at the surf's edge and in rock pools, best at night ([FM 21-76, p. 73](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=73)).
- Fish feed heavily before a storm and not after it, when the water is muddy and swollen; in a current they rest in eddies behind rocks, in deep pools, under overhanging bushes and around sunken logs; and at night a light draws them ([FM 21-76, p. 75](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=75)).

{{#unless shops}}
Fishing is the wild food that repays the effort when the shops are shut: shore fishing needs no licence, mackerel come inshore in shoals in summer and take almost anything shiny, and a hand line costs nothing and lives in a pocket ([Atlantic mackerel](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Atlantic_mackerel); [Handline fishing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Handline_fishing)). Set lines and traps fish while you work at something else, so check them morning and evening. Every hook, weight and metre of line is now irreplaceable: keep them in a tin, and learn the gorge and the improvised hook before you need them ([FM 21-76, p. 89](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=89)).
{{/unless}}

{{#if scenario:storms-flooding}}
**Not today.** Rivers rise within an hour of heavy rain, banks give way, and floodwater is sewage carrying leptospirosis, which usually starts like flu one to two weeks later and sometimes as much as a month ([Leptospirosis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Leptospirosis); [UKHSA flooding and health](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/flooding-and-health-public-advice/flooding-and-health-advice-for-the-public)). The shore is worse in a storm: the sea takes people off rocks, piers and harbour walls. Wait for the water to fall and clear, remember that fish feed before a storm and not after it, and leave shellfish alone while anything is being washed into the sea ([FM 21-76, p. 75](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=75)).
{{/if}}

## Hand line

A hand line is a single line held in the hands rather than on a rod, with one or more baited hooks, usually a weight and sometimes a float; you jig it to attract fish or fish it still and feel for the take ([Handline fishing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Handline_fishing)). It fits in a pocket wound on a bit of wood or a bottle, which is why it belongs in every bag. Hooks can be improvised from pins, needles, wire, small nails, thorns or bone; a gorge, a short double-pointed sliver of wood or bone notched in the middle and buried lengthways in the bait, turns sideways when the fish swallows it ([FM 21-76, p. 89](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=89)). Line can be braided from any strong thread or the inner strands of paracord.

## Set lines and stakeouts

A set line fishes while you do something else. A stakeout is two supple saplings driven into the bed of a lake or stream with their tops just under the surface, a cord tied between them just below the water, and two short baited droppers on it, tied so they cannot wrap round the poles or each other ([FM 21-76, p. 89](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=89)). A trotline or setline is a longer main cord with a series of short hooked droppers, weighted to keep the hooks below the surface and run out from the bank or between two anchors ([Trotline](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Trotline)). Check set lines often: a hooked fish left on the line is a meal for a pike, an otter or a gull.

## Nets

- A **gill net** hangs in the water like a curtain and catches fish by the gills as they try to swim through. One can be made from cord or the inner strands of paracord by tying rows of overhand knots in pairs along a top line strung between two trees, moving a guide line down each row to keep the mesh even ([FM 21-76, p. 90](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=90); [Gillnetting](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Gillnetting)).
- A **drop net or lift net** has its opening facing upwards: bait it, lower it to the bottom from a pier, bridge or rock, wait, and lift it fast. Hand nets held open by a hoop on a handle, and the lave net of the Severn, which the fisherman lifts when he feels a fish hit the mesh, work the same way ([Fishing net](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Fishing_net)). A drop net is the tool for crabs and prawns off a harbour wall.
- A **cast net** thrown over a shoal in shallow water needs practice but catches mullet and small fry ([Cast net](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Cast_net)).

## Basket and tidal traps

A fish basket is sticks lashed with cord or withies into a funnel with the wide mouth turned inward, so fish swim in and cannot find the way out; baited and set in a stream or an eel run overnight it is the oldest trap there is ([FM 21-76, p. 91](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=91); [Fish trap](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Fish_trap)). On the coast, shoals come in with the tide and move along the shore: pick the spot at high water and build at low water, a low stone wall running out from the beach at an angle, or block the mouth of a rock pool, and collect what is stranded as the tide falls ([FM 21-76, p. 92](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=92)).

## Gutting and keeping fish

Fish spoils fast, faster on a warm day. Cut out the gills and the big blood vessels along the spine, gut anything over 10 cm long, and scale or skin it as soon as it is caught; boiling with the skin on keeps the most food value, and the water becomes broth ([FM 21-76, p. 95](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=95)). Do not eat fish with sunken eyes, an odd smell, faded scales, slimy rather than wet skin, or flesh that holds a thumb dent: cooking does not make spoiled fish safe ([FM 21-76, p. 95](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=95)). Cook all freshwater fish through, for parasites ([FM 21-76, p. 75](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=75)); the sea worm Anisakis dies at over 60 °C or with freezing ([Anisakis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Anisakis)); and mackerel, herring and sardines left unchilled develop histamine that cooking does not remove, so eat or salt oily fish the day they are caught ([Scombroid food poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scombroid_food_poisoning)).

To keep fish: bacteria in the gut and gills multiply from the moment of death and soften the flesh in two to four days even on ice, so gut it and get it cold, dry, salted or smoked ([Fish preservation (VITA)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/FISHPROC.HTM)). For smoking, cut off the head and remove the backbone, salt first, then cold smoke at 28 to 32 °C for days or hot smoke at 70 to 80 °C, which cooks it ([FM 21-76, p. 95](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=95); [Smoked fish](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Smoked_fish)). Dry salting and wind drying were the only ways to keep fish until the nineteenth century and still work ([Salted fish](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Salted_fish)); the salting and smoking method for meat on the [butchery page](page:butchery) applies to fish.

{{#unless power}}
No fridge or freezer means the catch is eaten, salted, dried or smoked the day it is caught. Gut and gill it on the shore, keep it in the shade under a wet cloth, and have the salt or the smoker ready before you go out rather than after you come back ([Fish preservation (VITA)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/FISHPROC.HTM)). Oily fish are the ones that cannot wait: mackerel, herring and sardines left unchilled develop histamine that cooking does not remove ([Scombroid food poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scombroid_food_poisoning)).
{{/unless}}

## Shellfish, the tide and the toxins

Mussels, cockles, limpets, winkles and razor clams are the most reliable wild protein on the British shore; take them only from clean water away from outfalls and harbours, only alive and closed, cook them through, and discard any that stay shut ([Wild food in Britain and Ireland](page:fieldcraft-food)). Three rules on top of that:

- **Water.** Shellfish filter the water they sit in, and shellfish heated to under 75 °C are a high-risk food for norovirus; boil or steam them properly ([Norovirus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Norovirus)). Do not eat shellfish that are not covered by water at high tide ([FM 21-76, p. 74](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=74)).

- **Toxins.** In warm months algal blooms make filter-feeding shellfish poisonous. Paralytic shellfish poisoning (saxitoxin) starts within ten to thirty minutes with tingling lips and can paralyse breathing, and its toxins are stable to heat and acid, so cooking does not remove them ([Paralytic shellfish poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Paralytic_shellfish_poisoning)); amnesic shellfish poisoning (domoic acid) causes permanent memory loss and death ([Amnesic shellfish poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Amnesic_shellfish_poisoning)); diarrhetic shellfish poisoning starts in about half an hour and lasts a day ([Diarrhetic shellfish poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Diarrheic_shellfish_poisoning)). A red or brown tide, dead fish or birds on the tideline, or a local warning means leave the shellfish alone ([FM 21-76, p. 184](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=184)). Winkles boiled in seawater and limpets, tough and needing a long boil or roast, carry less filter-feeding risk than mussels and cockles but come under the same water rule ([Common periwinkle](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Common_periwinkle); [Common limpet](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Patella_vulgata)).
- **The tide.** The tide runs on a cycle of about 12 hours 25 minutes, two lows and two highs a day, each about 50 minutes later than the day before, and on sand flats and in estuaries it comes in faster than you can walk and fills the channels behind you first ([Tide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tide)). In 2004 at least 22 cockle pickers drowned on Morecambe Bay when the incoming tide cut them off ([Morecambe Bay cockling disaster](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Morecambe_Bay_cockling_disaster)). Go out on a falling tide, know the time of low water, turn back at low water whatever you have gathered, keep the shore in sight, and tell someone where you are going and when you will be back; how to read tide times is on [Map, compass and tides](page:fieldcraft-navigation).

{{#unless sewage}}
With the sewers not working, whatever the towns produce is reaching the water: take nothing filter-feeding from an estuary, a harbour or anywhere below a settlement, because shellfish concentrate what is in the water and are a high-risk food for norovirus unless heated properly; the estuary and outfall rule here is sense drawn from how shellfish waters are graded, not a quoted instruction ([Norovirus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Norovirus)). Fish caught away from outfalls, gutted with clean hands and cooked through, are the safer choice; wash your hands after handling anything from doubtful water and keep cuts covered ([Living in the field: hygiene](page:fieldcraft-hygiene)).
{{/unless}}
