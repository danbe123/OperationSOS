## The first rule

Breast milk is the safest food a baby can have in an emergency: it is clean, always the right temperature, carries antibodies against diarrhoea and other infections, and needs no water, fuel or bottles ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 320](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=320)). The World Health Organization recommends nothing but breast milk for the first six months and continued breastfeeding with other foods to two years and beyond ([Breastfeeding (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Breastfeeding)). A mother who is breastfeeding when the shops empty should keep going and feed more often, not ration it: milk supply follows demand, and a thin, tired or frightened mother still makes milk that is good for her baby ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Do not give extra water or teas, even in hot weather ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). A baby who is gaining weight and wetting nappies regularly is getting enough ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 320](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=320)).

## Relactation and wet nursing

- **Relactation** is restarting breastfeeding after it has stopped; mothers in poor countries do it routinely, and it has been demonstrated with varying success in adoptive mothers and wet nurses who have never been pregnant ([Breastfeeding](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Breastfeeding); [Lactation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lactation)). The method is the same as building supply: put the baby to the breast at every feed, at least every two hours, before any other food; and express by hand or pump at least eight times in 24 hours between feeds ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 320](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=320); [Low milk supply (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Low_milk_supply)). Give any other milk after the breast, by cup, and reduce it as the supply returns ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Expect days to weeks. Domperidone increases supply in some women but is prescription-only in the UK: ask a pharmacist or GP if they can be reached, and never buy it from an unregulated seller ([Low milk supply (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Low_milk_supply)).
- **Wet nursing and shared nursing**, another woman breastfeeding the baby, is how babies without a mother's milk survived for most of history and is still common in much of the world ([Breastfeeding (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Breastfeeding); [Wet nurse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wet_nurse)). The risk is infection passed in milk, HIV above all, so the wet nurse should be someone whose health you know: a relative or neighbour who is already breastfeeding is the obvious choice ([Wet nurse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wet_nurse); [Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Expressed milk from a known, healthy mother, given by cup, is the next best thing.

## When the formula runs out

In this order:

1. **Any infant formula**, whatever the brand or stage; first-stage formula suits any age under one year. Ask neighbours, the pharmacy, the church, the food bank, the health visitor: a tin found is a week bought.
2. **Breast milk** from the mother (relactation) or another woman (above).
3. **A home stop-gap for a baby under six months, for days not months.** Where There Is No Doctor gives two: 2 parts boiled and cooled cow's or goat's milk to 1 part boiled and cooled water, with a large spoonful of sugar or vegetable oil per large glass; or 2 parts evaporated (not condensed) milk to 3 parts boiled and cooled water with the same spoonful of sugar or oil; if the milk is skimmed, add another spoonful of oil ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Boil the milk and the water, feed by cup, and go back to formula or breast milk the moment you can. This is a bridge across a gap, not a diet.
4. **A baby over six months** can be kept going on a porridge of rice, oats or other cereal with mashed skinned beans, egg, meat or fish beaten in, with a little sugar and oil, alongside whatever milk there is; cereal water or rice water alone is not enough and a baby fed on it will not grow and may die ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)).

**Why plain cow's milk and carton "milks" are not safe under 12 months.** Cow's milk on its own does not have the balance of nutrients a baby needs, causes anaemia and gut bleeding, and its high casein and low whey are hard for an infant gut to digest, leading to diarrhoea and malnutrition; formula is cow's milk that has been altered to resemble breast milk and fortified with iron ([Infant food safety (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_food_safety); [Infant formula (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Infant_formula); [Infant feeding (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_feeding)). Oat, almond, rice, coconut and soya drinks from the supermarket are not infant formula and have none of that fortification; soya-based infant formula is a manufactured product, not the drink ([Infant formula (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Infant_formula)). Never give honey to a baby under one, and never water formula down to make it last: a hungry baby on thin feeds is worse off than one on fewer full ones.

If the baby has diarrhoea, keep breastfeeding or keep giving milk, and replace what is lost with oral rehydration solution in the amounts on the [dehydration card](card:dehydration) ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 205](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=205)).

## Making up formula without a kettle

Powdered formula is not sterile; it can carry Cronobacter, which in a small baby causes meningitis and sepsis, and the water used to make it up has to be hot enough to kill it: bring water to a rolling boil for one minute, let it cool to no lower than 70 °C, pour it into the bottle, add the powder, shake, then cool the bottle quickly under cold water or in a bowl of cold water and test a drop on your wrist ([Infant food safety (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_food_safety); [Cronobacter](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Cronobacter)). {{#if power}}A pan on the hob does what the kettle did.{{else}}With no power, a pan on a gas hob lit with a match or on a camping stove outdoors or in a well-ventilated room does what the kettle did; never a barbecue or stove indoors ([carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).{{/if}} Water straight off the boil, lidded, is still above 70 °C for a while; if you have a cooking thermometer use it, and do not let the water go cold before adding powder. Bottled water is not sterile and should be boiled too ([Infant food safety (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_food_safety)); water from a butt or stream must be treated as in [water disinfection](page:water-disinfection) and then boiled for the bottle. Make one feed at a time; a feed that must be kept goes in a fridge and is used within 24 hours, and anything left in the bottle an hour after a feed is thrown away ([Infant food safety (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_food_safety); [Formula feeding (MedlinePlus)](kiwix:medlineplus.gov_en_all/medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000806.htm)). Ready-to-feed liquid formula needs no water at all and is worth its weight in the emergency store.

{{#unless water}}
No tap does not lower the standard: the water for a feed is still brought to a rolling boil and used at no lower than 70 °C, because powdered formula is not sterile and Cronobacter causes meningitis and sepsis in a small baby ([Infant food safety (MDWiki)](kiwix:mdwiki_en_all_maxi/Infant_food_safety)). Water from a butt, a stream or a tanker is treated first and then boiled for the bottle ([water disinfection](page:water-disinfection)). Boiling is also the steriliser: at least ten minutes in a covered pan ([sterilising bottles (NHS)](kiwix:nhs_uk/www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/breastfeeding-and-bottle-feeding/bottle-feeding/sterilising-baby-bottles/)), and check teats for cracks, because boiling wears them faster. Set aside a share of the treated water for feeds and bottles before anything else uses it.
{{/unless}}

## Cup feeding and clean bottles

Bottles and teats are hard to keep clean and are the commonest way a baby catches diarrhoea; a cup, or a cup and spoon, is safer, and even a newborn can be fed from a small cup held to the lower lip so it laps ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169); [Where There Is No Doctor, p. 345](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=345)). Premature and sick babies are routinely fed expressed milk by cup, spoon or syringe in hospital ([Breastfeeding (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Breastfeeding)). If you use a bottle with no steriliser: wash bottle, teat and cup in hot soapy water with a bottle brush straight after the feed, rinse, then boil them in a covered pan of water for 5 minutes and leave them covered until the next feed ([Formula feeding (MedlinePlus)](kiwix:medlineplus.gov_en_all/medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000806.htm); [Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Boiling is the steriliser. Wash your hands before every feed.

{{#if scenario:pandemic}}
A mother who is ill keeps breastfeeding: her milk carries antibodies to what she has, and stopping costs the baby that protection as well as the supply ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 320](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=320); [Breastfeeding (WikiMed)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Breastfeeding)). Wash your hands before every feed, keep a mask or cloth over your face while feeding if you are coughing, and if she is too ill to feed, express by hand and let someone well give it by cup, which is the standard WHO and UNICEF practice for feeding in emergencies rather than NHS consumer advice ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)). Choose a wet nurse whose health you know ([Wet nurse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wet_nurse)).
{{/if}}

## When to get help

A baby who will not feed, is floppy or unusually sleepy, has fewer wet nappies, has a fever, or has diarrhoea or vomiting for more than a day needs a clinician: {{#if phones}}[[call 111]], or in Northern Ireland the GP out-of-hours service ([UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)), or [[call 999]] if the baby is limp, blue or breathing fast ([fever in a child](card:fever-child); [dehydration](card:dehydration)).{{else}}with the phones down, take the baby to the nearest open pharmacy, GP or hospital in person ([getting help without phones](page:no-phones); [fever in a child](card:fever-child); [dehydration](card:dehydration)).{{/if}} Where There Is No Doctor's chapters on feeding and on children are written for exactly the situation where nobody is coming ([Where There Is No Doctor, p. 169](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=169)).
