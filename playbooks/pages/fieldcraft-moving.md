## Where you may go

England and Wales: public footpaths, bridleways and byways (the green dashes on the map) and open access land (the yellow-shaded moor, heath, down and registered common) ([CROW Act](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Countryside_and_Rights_of_Way_Act_2000)). Scotland: almost everywhere, responsibly, including wild camping ([Scottish Outdoor Access Code](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scottish_Outdoor_Access_Code)). Ireland: very little public right of way; walk on marked trails, hill land by custom and with permission. The offline map shows footpaths and access land as overlays ([Footpaths and access land on the map](map:?overlay=footpaths&overlay=access-land)). Trespass in England and Wales is a civil matter, not a crime, unless you are on railways, military land, or damaging things, or the trespass is aggravated; in an evacuation nobody will care, but farmers with livestock and crops will, and a gate left open can lose someone a herd.

## How fast you actually move

Naismith's rule: five kilometres an hour on the flat plus one minute for every ten metres of ascent, and a tired party with packs does two thirds of that ([Naismith's rule](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Naismith's_rule)). A family with children and a pram does two kilometres an hour on lanes and less across fields. Bog, tussock, bracken and heather halve any speed. In the dark, halve it again. Set out with a target you can reach in daylight with an hour to spare, and know where the next shelter is before you leave the last ([Navigation module](module:navigation)).

## Rivers

British rivers are small until it rains, and then they are not. A beck you stepped over in the morning can be waist-deep and killing by the evening; upland rivers rise within an hour of heavy rain and fall again a day later. The first rule is to wait or go round: a bridge, a ford marked on the map, or the headwaters. If you must cross:

- Choose a wide, shallow, slow stretch with a clean run-out below, never above a weir, rapid or fallen tree.
- Undo the waist belt and chest strap so you can shed the pack; keep boots on.
- Face upstream, lean on a pole, shuffle sideways, feet apart, one foot moving at a time.
- Groups: link arms in a line across the current, or in a huddle, the strongest upstream; or use a rope, but never tie it to anyone.
- Never cross fast water above the knee, cold water above the thigh, or anything you cannot see the bottom of.

If you are swept away: feet up and downstream, on your back, steer to the bank, and get out of wet clothes at once ([Cold shock](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Cold_shock_response); [Military Mountaineering, TC 3-97.61, the river-crossing chapter](doc:tc-3-97-61-military-mountaineering)).

## Bog, moor and mountain

Bright green sphagnum, cotton grass and pools mean deep bog; heather and bilberry mean firm ground. Test with a pole, walk on the tussocks, and if you sink to the thigh, spread your weight backwards and crawl out ([Bog](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bog)). Peat hags, the eroded trenches of the Pennines and the Cairngorms, hide you from the wind and swallow hours. On the tops, the danger is the weather and the cliff in the mist: in poor visibility, walk on a bearing, count paces, and treat every edge as a drop ([Reading the weather and exposure](page:fieldcraft-weather)).

## The coast

Britain has some of the largest tides in the world. Sands and estuaries such as Morecambe Bay, the Wash, the Solway and the Severn flood faster than a person can run, through channels that fill behind you first ([Morecambe Bay](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Morecambe_Bay); [Tide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tide)). Never walk out on open sands without local tide times and a route; never round a headland on the beach without knowing when the tide turns; keep off cliff edges and cliff bases, which fall without warning. In the sea, rip currents run out through gaps in the surf: swim across them, not against ([Rip current](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Rip_current)). The coastguard, not the police, is the 999 service on the shore ([Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue)).

## Livestock

Cattle kill a few walkers every year, nearly always cows with calves defending them from a dog. Keep dogs on a lead near stock, let the dog go if cattle charge, walk quietly round the edge of the field, never between a cow and her calf, never turn your back and run. Bulls of dairy breeds may not be kept in fields with public paths; beef bulls with cows may. Horses are curious and heavy; sheep are harmless; pigs and boar are not to be cornered ([Cattle](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Cattle); [Wild boar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wild_boar)).

## At night

Move at night only with a reason: heat, pursuit, or a road you dare not use by day. Red light keeps night vision; a head torch on full ruins it for twenty minutes. Follow linear features, walls, tracks, rivers and field edges, count paces, and stop every hour to listen. A British night in summer never gets fully dark in the north; in winter it lasts sixteen hours. Fog on low ground and moor is the real hazard: in fog, stop.

## Carrying

Fifteen to twenty kilograms is a sustainable load for a fit adult on tracks for days; a third of body weight is the limit for anyone. Weight high and close to the back, hip belt taking most of it, water and waterproofs on top, the heaviest things nearest the spine. A wheelbarrow, a pram, a bicycle pushed with panniers, or a sledge on snow moves far more than a back does, and the roads and canal towpaths of Britain are made for wheels ([Evacuation module](module:evacuation); [Vehicles and fuel module](module:vehicles-fuel)).
