## Where our weather comes from

Britain sits under the track of Atlantic depressions. Most bad weather arrives from the south-west and west, in a pattern you can read a day ahead:

- **A front is coming**: high wispy cloud from the west thickening to a milky sheet, a halo round the sun or moon, the wind backing (turning anticlockwise, say from west to south), a falling barometer, a warm damp feel. Rain within six to twelve hours, then wind.
- **The front has passed**: the wind veers (clockwise) to west or north-west, the temperature drops, the sky clears to blue with showers building into towers by afternoon. Showers, gusts, cold nights.
- **Settled weather**: a slowly rising or steady barometer, light winds, mist in the mornings, dew at night, and in winter frost and fog that may not lift all day.
- **Red sky**: at sunset, fair tomorrow is usually right in Britain because the clear air is to the west; at sunrise it means the clear spell has already passed east and rain is on its way.

A cheap barometer or a watch with one is the most useful forecasting tool without the internet. The [radio module](module:comms) covers where to hear forecasts when the internet is down; the shipping forecast and the coastal stations are still broadcast on long wave.

## Hills multiply everything

Temperature drops about 1°C per 100 m climbed, wind speed doubles between valley and summit, and cloud sits on the tops when the valleys are clear. Rain that is a nuisance at 100 m is a killer at 800 m in wind. The Beaufort scale on the ground: force 6 (whole trees moving, hard to walk into) is the limit for comfortable movement on ridges, force 8 (twigs breaking off, cannot walk against it) means get off the hill ([Beaufort scale](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Beaufort_scale)).

## Wet cold: the British killer

Wind chill is the temperature the skin feels: 5°C in a 40 km/h wind feels like minus 3, and wet skin and clothing make it far worse ([Wind chill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wind_chill)). Hypothermia in Britain happens at 5 to 12°C to wet, tired, underfed people in wind, more often in summer than in deep winter because they set out unprepared. Signs, treatment and prevention are on the [shelter page](page:fieldcraft-shelter) and the [hypothermia card](card:hypothermia). Frostbite is rare here outside the Scottish winter; non-freezing cold injury of wet feet over days is common in any British winter and is prevented with dry socks and feet out of boots at night ([Frostbite](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Frostbite); [Trench foot](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Trench_foot)).

## Cold water

British seas are 6 to 18°C and rivers and lakes colder. Falling in causes cold shock: an uncontrollable gasp and a minute of panicked breathing that drowns strong swimmers in the first minute. The rule is **float first**: lie back, arms out, let the gasping pass, then swim or call. Ten minutes later the hands and arms stop working; after an hour, unconsciousness. Get the casualty out horizontal if you can, strip and insulate, handle gently ([Cold shock response](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Cold_shock_response); [Drowning card](card:drowning)).

## Lightning

Storms build on summer afternoons and along fronts. When thunder follows the flash by less than thirty seconds, the strike is within ten kilometres and you are in range. Get off summits, ridges and open ground; away from lone trees, fences, wire and water; not into a shallow cave or under an overhang. In the open, crouch on a mat or pack with feet together and hands off the ground, spread the group out, and wait thirty minutes after the last thunder ([Lightning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lightning)). A struck person is safe to touch: start CPR if needed ([Adult CPR card](card:cpr-adult)).

## Heat, which is now real

The country hit 40°C in July 2022 and will again. Heat kills the old, the young, the ill and anyone working hard in it. Move in the early morning and evening, rest in shade at midday, drink before thirst and add salt to food, wear a hat and light long sleeves, and watch for heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, headache, cramps, nausea, faintness) before it becomes heat stroke (hot dry skin, confusion, collapse), which is a 999 emergency ([Heat stroke card](card:heat-stroke); [Heat and drought playbook](playbook:heat-drought)). Sunburn is a real injury: cover up, and the British sun burns through thin cloud.

## Fog, mist and dark

More people die on British hills from a fall in cloud than from cold. In mist, navigate by compass bearing and pace count, and if you cannot, stop, shelter and wait; a night out in a shelter beats a fall over an edge. Low-ground fog in winter can last days and turns roads and rivers lethal ([Navigation module](module:navigation)).

## Deciding

Before leaving any shelter: what is the forecast, what is the turn-back time, what is the escape route down and out of the wind, who knows the plan. The strongest field-craft skill in Britain is the decision to stay where you are.
