## Scrap comes first, by decades

Britain is made of steel. Vehicles, machinery, fencing, railings, girders, rails, springs, files, bearings and hand tools are lying about in quantities no community will exhaust in a lifetime, and every one of them is better steel than a first attempt at smelting will produce. Mining and smelting ore is a project for the far future; recovering, sorting and re-forging what is already made is the work of the first generation ([Steel](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Steel); [Steel recycling](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Steel_recycling)).

Learn to tell the stock apart, because the same fire treats them differently:

- **Leaf and coil springs** from vehicles: medium-carbon spring steel, the classic stock for knives, chisels, froes and hoes.
- **Files, rasps and old chisels**: high carbon, hard, and often the best blade steel you will find.
- **Bearing races and ball bearings**: high carbon, hardenable, awkward to forge because they are hard to begin with.
- **Rebar, angle iron, sheet, nails and most fencing**: mild steel of unknown make. It forges beautifully and will not harden. Use it for anything that does not need an edge — hinges, hooks, straps, brackets, nails.
- **Rail, axles and shafting**: medium carbon, heavy, excellent for anvils and for tooling.

Scrap is unknown alloy, so treat every new source as an experiment: forge a small coupon, harden and temper it as below, and break it in the vice to see whether it hardened at all and how coarse the grain is. Write the result in the log against the source, and you will build up a table of what your local scrap actually does ([Heat treatment processes for ferrous metals](kiwix:engineering.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions/3736/heat-treatment-processes-for-ferrous-metals); [Restarting science](page:rebuild-restarting-science)).

**Never put galvanised, plated or painted metal in the fire.** Heated zinc coating gives off fumes that cause a genuine, unpleasant poisoning, and old plating may be cadmium, which is worse. Grind or acid-strip the coating outdoors first, or use the piece cold.

## The forge

A working forge is a hearth, a hole in it for air, a way to move that air, and fuel. The simplest is a shallow bed of clay or a lined brake drum with a pipe entering from the side or the bottom — the tuyere — fed by a hand bellows, a hand-cranked blower or, while the electricity lasts, a hairdryer with a valve to throttle it ([Forge](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Forge); [Tuyere](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tuyere); [Bellows](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bellows)). Charcoal is the fuel that comes back first and it burns clean and hot; making it is on [Making things again](page:rebuild-making-things). Coal, where you have it, gives a hotter and more concentrated fire but needs coking and tending.

You also need: an anvil, which can be a length of rail or a heavy block of any steel set end-up in a stump; a cross-pein hammer of about a kilogram; tongs that fit the stock, which is the first thing to make; a bucket of water and a can of oil for quenching; and a vice. Work in daylight or a dim shop, because you are reading the colour of the steel and bright light makes that impossible.

**The forge makes carbon monoxide.** Work outdoors or in a building with a chimney and standing ventilation, never in a closed garage or shed ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).

## Reading heat by colour

Steel begins to glow visibly at about 525 °C, so anything below that is black heat and you cannot judge it by eye ([Draper point](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Draper_point)). Above that the colour is your thermometer: dull red, then cherry red, orange, yellow, and finally a sparkling near-white. Work the metal between yellow and orange and stop hammering when it falls to dull red, or you will crack it; put it back in the fire instead. Forge welding takes the top of the range — common steel of 0.2 to 0.8% carbon welds at a bright yellow heat, pure iron at nearly white, between about 1,400 and 1,500 °C, with a flux such as borax or clean sand to keep the scale out of the joint ([Forge welding](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Forge_welding)).

The other thermometer costs nothing: a magnet. Steel stops being magnetic at its Curie point, 770 °C for iron, which is close to the temperature you want for hardening, so heat until a magnet no longer sticks, give it a shade more, and quench from there ([Curie temperature](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Curie_temperature); [Heat treating](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Heat_treating)).

## Moving metal

Four operations do almost everything. **Drawing out** makes a bar longer and thinner, hammering over the far edge of the anvil or with the pein and then smoothing flat. **Upsetting** makes it shorter and fatter, hammering on the end of a hot bar stood on the anvil. **Bending** is done over the edge or the horn, hot, in one heat where you can. **Punching and drifting** makes a hole — punch most of the way from one side, turn over, punch through into the hardy hole, then open the hole to size with a tapered drift, which is far stronger than drilling ([Blacksmith](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Blacksmith); [Down'n'dirty Blacksmithing (Wikibooks)](kiwix:wikibooks_en_all_maxi/Down'n'dirty_Blacksmithing); [Blacksmithing (Appropedia)](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Blacksmithing); [Blacksmith's manual (CD3WD)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/CD3WD/APPRTECH/T0021E/INDEX.HTM)).

Anneal a piece you need to file or drill by heating it to red and burying it in dry ash or lime to cool overnight; normalise a finished forging by heating to non-magnetic and letting it cool in still air two or three times, which refines the grain the hammering coarsened ([Annealing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Annealing_(materials_science)); [Normalisation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Normalization_(metallurgy))).

## Hardening and tempering

Hardening and tempering are two halves of one job, and the second half is not optional: fully hardened steel is glass, and an untempered edge chips or a tool snaps.

**Harden.** Heat evenly to just past non-magnetic and quench, moving the piece edge-first through the liquid and keeping it moving. Warm oil — vegetable or engine oil at about hand-hot — is the right quench for most scrap, particularly springs, and is far less likely to crack the work; water and brine are faster and are for simple high-carbon steel where oil will not harden it, at the price of cracked blades ([Quenching](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Quenching)). Test with a file: if the file skates, it hardened.

**Temper straight away**, within the hour. Polish a face of the piece bright, then heat it gently — in an oven, on a hot plate, or by laying it on a thick bar heated in the forge — and watch the oxide colours run along the steel from the thick end towards the edge. Pull it and quench when the right colour reaches where you want it ([Tempering](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tempering_(metallurgy)); [Tempering carbon steel](kiwix:engineering.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions/39963/tempering-1095-steel)):

- **Faint yellow, about 176 °C**: razors, gravers, scrapers.
- **Light straw, about 205 °C**: drills, reamers, metal-cutting saws.
- **Dark straw, about 226 °C**: scribers, planer blades.
- **Brown, about 260 °C**: taps, dies, twist drills, hammers, cold chisels.
- **Purple, about 282 °C**: punches, stone tools, surgical instruments.
- **Dark blue, about 310 °C**: screwdrivers, spanners.
- **Light blue, about 337 °C**: springs.

A kitchen or wood-fired oven that holds 200 to 230 °C for an hour tempers a blade more evenly than colours do, and colour is the fallback when you have no oven.

## What to make, in order

**A knife.** Anneal a leaf spring or a worn file, cut a blank, forge the bevels roughly, normalise, grind or file to shape leaving the edge a coin's thickness, harden, then temper to light straw at the edge and let the spine run to blue. Fit a wooden handle with pins or a peened tang. Do not thin the edge fully before hardening, or it will crack in the quench.

**Nails.** Mild steel, drawn to a taper in a few heats, cut most of the way through, snapped off in the hardy, dropped into a heading tool or a hole in the anvil and the head upset with three blows. A morning makes a hundred, and nails buy things ([Nail](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Nail_(fastener))).

**Hinges, straps, hasps and hooks.** Mild steel, punched and drifted, no heat treatment. These are the everyday work and they hold buildings together ([Hinge](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hinge)).

**A hoe, a mattock, a billhook.** Spring steel, eye punched and drifted rather than welded where you can, edge hardened and tempered to dark straw or brown so it takes a knock without chipping.

**A ploughshare.** The share and coulter are the wearing parts of the plough and the reason a village needs a smith at all: they are re-pointed, re-laid and re-hardened every season, and a plough that cannot be maintained stops the ploughing ([Plough](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Plough)).

## Sharpening

Everything is sharpened the same way: establish the bevel on a coarse stone, refine it on a fine one, work until a burr you can feel with a thumb runs the whole length of the edge, then turn the tool over and take the burr off, finishing on a strop of leather ([Sharpening stone](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Sharpening_stone)). Keep the angle constant and let the stone do the work. A shallow angle cuts better and dulls sooner; a steeper one lasts on rough work. Water or oil on the stone floats the swarf away and keeps the stone cutting.

## Tools for wood

Green woodworking needs a small kit and gives you handles, spoons, bowls, chairs, gates, shingles and buckets: a **froe** to split billets along the grain, a **drawknife** used towards you on a **shave horse**, an **adze** for hollowing and for squaring beams, a felling axe and a broad axe, a brace and bits, and a good saw ([Froe](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Froe); [Drawknife](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Drawknife); [Adze](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Adze)). Split wood rather than sawing it wherever the job allows: split timber follows the grain and is stronger, and it costs no blade. Working practice and repair are in the [Tools and repair module](module:tools-repair).

## The bloomery, for the far future

When the scrap does finally run down — generations away — iron comes from ore without ever melting it. Roast the ore, in Britain most likely bog iron, to drive off water and break it up; build a clay shaft furnace a couple of metres tall; charge it with alternating charcoal and ore with forced air from bellows; and run it for most of a day. Carbon monoxide from the charcoal reduces the ore at temperatures below iron's melting point of about 1,538 °C, leaving a spongy mass of iron and slag — the bloom — at the bottom, which is then hammered hot, repeatedly, to squeeze out the slag and consolidate it into wrought iron ([Bloomery](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bloomery); [Wrought iron](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wrought_iron)). It is a multi-day job for several people and eats charcoal, which is why it is the last step and not the first. Read it now, so that somebody knows it is possible.

The extended library also carries the Survivor Library, some fifty thousand scanned pre-1920s trade books, including blacksmithing, farriery, edge-tool making and forge practice from the era when all of this was ordinary work; it is worth copying before it is needed.
