## The arithmetic that decides everything

Start with the sum, because it settles how much land a village needs and how many people a valley holds. **An adult doing farm work needs roughly 3,000 kcal a day, about 1.1 million kcal a year.** The familiar figures of 2,000 kcal for a woman and 2,500 for a man are average-activity numbers, not digging numbers, and cold winters, nursing mothers and growing teenagers push the requirement higher again ([Food storage](page:food-storage); [Food energy](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Food_energy); [Physical activity level](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Physical_activity_level)). Plan on 3,000 and you will be roughly right; plan on 2,000 and you will be a third short.

Potatoes are the most efficient staple by area, but by less than the folklore says. Work it from the harvest figures rather than from a slogan. World potato yield is about 22.8 tonnes a hectare (390 million tonnes off 17.1 million hectares in 2024) at roughly 3.2 MJ a kilogram, which is about **73 gigajoules a hectare**, or 7.1 million kcal an acre ([Potato](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Potato); [Food energy](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Food_energy)). World wheat yield is about 3.6 tonnes a hectare (799 million tonnes off 219.5 million hectares) at about 13.7 MJ a kilogram, which is about **50 GJ a hectare**, or 4.8 million kcal an acre ([Wheat](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wheat); [Food energy](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Food_energy)). At 3,000 kcal a day, an acre of potatoes at world-average yields carries about six working adults for a year and an acre of wheat about four and a half. Potatoes lead, but by half again, not threefold. That is arithmetic on published yields before any loss, not a promise.

Now take the modern fertiliser away. Those yields assume industrial nitrogen; the UK's average wheat yield of about 8 tonnes a hectare is a Haber-process number ([Wheat](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wheat); [Haber process](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Haber_process)). The best that English farming managed on muck, marl and clover was about 30 bushels an acre by the 1890s, roughly 2,080 kg a hectare — a quarter of today's — and in the 1300s it was nearer 8 bushels ([British Agricultural Revolution](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/British_Agricultural_Revolution)). So plan on a quarter to a third of modern yields in the first years, rising towards a half once the leys and the muck are working, then take off seed held back, storage losses, and the fact that in a four-course rotation only part of the ground is in a staple in any one year.

The working figure that comes out of that: **budget roughly an acre of cropped ground per person, and hold as much again in ley, grass and hay — call it two acres a head all told**, and then correct it against what your own ground actually produces in year one. Write the harvest down field by field. In two years your own numbers beat anything on this page.

Two more rules the arithmetic hides. First, never live on one crop: Ireland in the 1840s lost a population to a single potato disease ([Great Famine (Ireland)](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Great_Famine_(Ireland))), and that blight is still endemic wherever potatoes are grown here, overwintering in tubers and dumps ([Phytophthora infestans](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Phytophthora_infestans)). Grow potatoes **and** a grain **and** beans. Second, beans are not optional: they carry the protein that potatoes and grain are short of, and they put nitrogen back ([Vicia faba](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Vicia_faba); [Phaseolus vulgaris](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Phaseolus_vulgaris)).

## Rotation

Rotation is what stops soil disease building up and what keeps fertility circulating without a fertiliser lorry ([Crop rotation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Crop_rotation)). The pattern to copy is the Norfolk four-course that carried English farming for two centuries: wheat, then turnips or another root, then barley, usually undersown with clover and ryegrass, then the clover ley grazed or cut before the wheat comes round again ([Norfolk four-course system](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Norfolk_four-course_system)). Roots and the clover ley are the fertility; the animals that eat them are the muck cart.

At garden scale the same idea works over three or four beds: potatoes, then legumes, then brassicas, then roots ([Growing food module](module:growing-food)). Hold the rotation even when one bed is tempting, and avoid two grain crops off the same ground in a row: a second wheat is not impossible, it simply yields less and carries the disease forward into the next one.

## Fertility with nothing bought

Everything that leaves the field has to come back to it.

- **Muck.** Every animal on the holding is a fertiliser works: bedding, dung and urine composted and spread ([Manure](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Manure); [Livestock module](module:livestock)).
- **Compost.** Every scrap of plant waste, hot-heaped, turned, and returned to the beds ([Compost](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Compost)).
- **Legumes and green manures.** Clover, vetch, beans and peas fix nitrogen from the air, adding somewhere between 45 and 224 kg of nitrogen a hectare depending on the crop, of which 40 to 60% is available to the crop that follows ([Green manure](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Green_manure); [Nitrogen fixation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Nitrogen_fixation)).
- **Urine, ash and comfrey.** Urine diluted about one part in eight to ten is a free nitrogen feed, and neat urine burns leaves and roots, so it is always watered down and watered in; wood ash returns potash to the soil and is also caustic, so keep it off seedlings and out of eyes ([Urine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Urine); [Wood ash](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wood_ash); [Symphytum](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Symphytum)).

**Night soil, safely or not at all.** Human waste is the largest nutrient stream a village throws away, and it is also how ascariasis and every faecal-oral disease spreads; the risk is real and documented ([Night soil](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Night_soil); [Sanitation module](module:sanitation)). The rules are not negotiable: material taken straight from a compost chamber, or composted only at ambient temperature, **is not safe for food production**. Helminth eggs are the hardiest pathogens of all, and killing them needs **thermophilic composting held at 55 °C for at least two weeks, or at 60 °C for one week**, measured with a probe pushed into the middle of the heap and not at its edge — or, where you cannot reach those temperatures, **one to two full years of storage with nothing fresh added** ([Composting toilet](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Composting_toilet)). Forty degrees is mesophilic and will not kill helminth eggs, so a heap that never gets properly hot is a storage heap and must be timed as one. Use finished, aged material on grass, orchards and fibre crops, and **never on salad or any crop eaten raw, whatever the heap reached**. Urine kept separate carries far less disease than faeces, but it is not sterile — not even in the bladder — and it can carry typhoid, leptospirosis and schistosomiasis ([Urine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Urine)). Store it a month in a closed drum before it goes on anything, water it into the soil and keep it off leaves.

## Seed, and the village seed bank

A crop you cannot re-sow is a crop with one year left. Save open-pollinated varieties, never F1 hybrids, which do not breed true ([Open pollination](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Open_pollination); [F1 hybrid](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/F1_hybrid); [Seed saving](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seed_saving)).

Two things decide whether saved seed comes true: the separation distance from other varieties of the same species, and growing enough plants to keep the variety's genetic diversity ([Seed saving](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seed_saving)). Know what crosses with what:

- **The brassicas are one species.** Cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprout, kohlrabi and collard are all cultivars of *Brassica oleracea*, so any two of them flowering near each other will cross ([Brassica oleracea](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Brassica_oleracea)). Let one of them run to seed in a season, not two, keep about a mile between it and any other flowering *B. oleracea*, and save from at least a dozen plants — the figures the Real Seed Catalogue's seed-saving notes give. Turnip, swede and the oriental greens are *Brassica rapa* and *Brassica napus* and will **not** cross with these ([Brassica rapa](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Brassica_rapa); [Brassica napus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Brassica_napus)).
- **Beetroot, chard, mangelwurzel and sugar beet are one species too** (*Beta vulgaris*), and they are wind-pollinated, so they need real distance — around two miles from any other flowering beet ([Beta vulgaris](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Beta_vulgaris)).
- **Carrots cross with the wild carrot in the hedge**, because the cultivated carrot and Queen Anne's lace are the same species ([Daucus carota](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Daucus_carota)).
- **Maize is wind-pollinated** and will cross with any other maize in the district ([Maize](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Maize)). Where distance is impossible, separate in time instead: stagger the sowings so the two crops are not shedding pollen in the same fortnight.
- **The easy ones** are the self-pollinators: peas, French beans, tomatoes and lettuce mostly come true with modest separation, which is why they are the crops to start seed-saving with.

Dry seed hard, label it with variety and year, and keep it cool, dark and dry. Hold two years of every important crop, and split the store between two houses so that one damp shed or one fire does not end a variety. Seed longevity varies sharply: parsnip, onion and leek seed is close to worthless after a year or two however well it is kept, so those are re-grown every year rather than banked. Run it as a bank with a ledger: who took what, who owes seed back after harvest ([Seed bank](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seed_bank); [Appropedia seed banks](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/GEN_Solution_Library/Seed_bank); [Community module](module:community)). Ask the gardening Q&A the specific questions as they come up ([Gardening Q&A](kiwix:gardening.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions)).

## Draught animals and hand tools

A person digs; a horse or an ox ploughs. One draught animal turns the ground a family cannot, and pulls the cart, the harrow and the mill drive as well ([Draft horse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Draft_horse); [Ox](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ox); [Plough](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Plough)). The cost is real: it must be fed all year off ground that would otherwise feed people, and harness, shoeing and a knowledgeable handler all have to exist.

The hand tools that matter are few and worth keeping sharp: spade, fork, hoe, rake, sickle and scythe, billhook, axe, and a means of sharpening ([Scythe](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scythe); [Tools and repair module](module:tools-repair)). Grain is cut, bound, stooked to dry, then threshed with flails on a hard floor and winnowed in a draught ([Threshing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Threshing); [Winnowing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Winnowing)), and milled at a water or wind mill ([Gristmill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Gristmill); [Iron and tools](page:rebuild-iron-and-tools); [Power from scratch](page:rebuild-power)).

## Storing a year, not a week

Harvest is only half the job. Grain must be dried to 12 to 13% moisture before it goes into store — below 13% mites cannot breed, and above 14.5% the food-safety risk climbs, so treat 14.5% as the hard line and dry immediately at 18% or more — put into containers cleaned of every trace of last year's grain, dust and insects, and kept cool and protected from swings in temperature; check sacks every week or two for heating, mould smell and dark kernels ([Grain storage, VITA](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/GRNSTOR3.HTM); [Grain drying](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Grain_drying)). Rats and mice eat more of a bad store than any other pest.

Roots go into a clamp or a cool cellar: a bed of straw, the crop heaped and covered with straw and soil, drainage round it, and only sound undamaged roots put in ([Root cellar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Root_cellar)). Beans and peas are dried in the pod. The rest is drying, salting, smoking and fermenting, plus hay and straw for the animals through winter ([Food drying](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Food_drying); [Hay](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hay); [Food storage](page:food-storage); [Butchery](page:butchery)). Set next year's seed and next year's seed potatoes aside **before** anyone eats this year's crop.

## The British season

Britain gives you rain, grass and a long mild autumn, and takes away heat and light. Nothing tender goes out until your own last frost has passed — late May in the south, and well into June in the north and on high ground; hardy crops, cloches and cold frames fill the rest ([Growing food module](module:growing-food); [Growing season](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Growing_season)). The trap is the hungry gap, April to early June, when last year's store is finished and this year's crop is not ready: it starts as the overwintered brassicas bolt and ends when the first broad beans come, and it is the stretch to plan stores and winter greens around ([Hungry gap](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hungry_gap)). A traditional 10-rod allotment, about 250 square metres, was reckoned to keep a family of four in *vegetables* for a year ([Allotment](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Allotment_(gardening))). That is not the same as feeding them: 250 m² is a sixteenth of an acre, so the staple calories still need the acres above, and the allotment supplies the greens and the vitamins on top of them.

## The animals

Hens, rabbits, goats and bees are the small livestock a village can carry, and the detail — registration, water needs, feed and welfare law — is in the [livestock module](module:livestock). Three rules survive from it into a long rebuild. Feed animals only what people cannot eat: grain fed to a hen returns less energy than it cost, so feed hens on forage, insects, spoiled roots and milling waste and keep the sound grain for people — though eggs do supply protein and fat that grain does not. Kitchen scraps are illegal feed in any case ([Chickens and ducks, VITA](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/CHICDUCK.HTM)). Keep the breeding line: a flock with no male is a flock with one generation left, so write down who is out of whom. And with no vet coming, prevention is all of veterinary medicine — dry housing, clean water, room to move, and a sick animal separated early ([WikiVet](kiwix:wikivet.net_en_all/New_Front_Page)).
