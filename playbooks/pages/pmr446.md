## The rules

PMR446 radios are licence-free across the UK and most of Europe: 0.5 W effective radiated power, a fixed antenna, 16 analogue channels 12.5 kHz apart between 446.00625 and 446.19375 MHz ([PMR446](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/PMR446); [Ofcom IR 2030, table 3.18](doc:ofcom-pmr446-ir2009#page=120)). Digital PMR446 (dPMR446 and DMR446) shares the same 446.0 to 446.2 MHz band. Range is one to three kilometres in a town and ten or more from a hilltop to a valley. No repeaters, no external antennas, no power boosting: a modified set is an unlicensed transmitter.

## Using them in an emergency

- Agree a channel and tone with your street or village and write it on the household plan ([Household plan](page:household-plan)); keep a second channel as a fallback. Some walking and volunteer groups suggest channel 8 with tone 16 as a listening channel, but that is an informal convention: nobody monitors it, so do not rely on it.
- Keep messages short: who you are, where you are, what you need. Say "over" when you finish and "out" when you are done.
- Listen before you transmit; a call every 15 minutes at an agreed time saves batteries.
- Rechargeable sets die in a long outage; sets that take AA cells keep going with a torch's batteries. Keep radios warm; cold kills batteries.
- PMR446 is for local coordination. Reaching further means licence-free CB on 27 MHz or a licensed amateur station; both are on [Amateur bands](page:amateur-bands). Listening to any of them needs no licence.

{{#unless mobile}}
These sets are now the household's telephone, so use them like one: agree the channel and tone with the street, keep to a listening schedule rather than leaving them on, and say who you are, where you are and what you need in one short transmission. Range is one to three kilometres in a town and ten or more from a hilltop, so a set carried up the hill or held at an upstairs window reaches households that one indoors cannot ([PMR446](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/PMR446); [getting help without phones](page:no-phones)).
{{/unless}}

{{#unless internet}}
There is nothing to look up: the channel and tone tables on this page are the reference, so copy the channel and tone your street has agreed onto the paper household plan and into the radios' memories now ([Household plan](page:household-plan)). Nobody is monitoring any channel on your behalf, and the "channel 8, tone 16" habit is only an informal convention, so an agreed time to listen is the one thing that gets a message heard.
{{/unless}}

{{#if scenario:emp}}
A handheld that was unplugged, and better still one that sat in a metal tin, is among the equipment most likely to have come through, while anything left on a mains charger or an outside aerial is the likely loss ([Nuclear electromagnetic pulse](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Nuclear_electromagnetic_pulse); [EMP](playbook:emp)). Test on receive first, keep to short check-ins at agreed times to save cells, and favour the sets that take AA cells, which a torch's batteries will keep going, over rechargeables with no mains to charge them.
{{/if}}

{{#if scenario:solar-storm}}
These are the radios that keep working: PMR446 is UHF and does not use the ionosphere, so it is unaffected while HF shortwave is blacked out and satellite services are unreliable ([Solar storm](playbook:solar-storm); [Amateur bands](page:amateur-bands)). The mobile network may fail on GPS timing before it fails on power, so move the street onto the agreed channel early, keep an FM radio for national news, and fix a check-in time so the sets can stay off in between ([What still works](page:what-still-works)).
{{/if}}
