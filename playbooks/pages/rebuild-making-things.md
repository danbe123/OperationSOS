## The order they come back in

Materials come back in a fixed order, because each one is the tool for the next. Soap comes first: it is the cheapest health measure there is and it needs only ash and fat. Charcoal comes next, because it is the only fuel that will reach forge and kiln temperatures. Lime needs that heat and gives mortar, plaster, limewash and the strong alkali that improves soap. Pottery needs the same fire and gives you vessels. Glass needs a hotter, longer-held fire than any of them, which is why it comes late. Around that spine sit alcohol, paper, cloth, salt and preserving, each of which can start at any point once there is fuel and a pot.

## Soap and lye

Leach wood ash — hardwood is best — with soft water in a barrel with a false bottom and draw off the brown liquor beneath. That is ash lye, mostly potassium carbonate — alkaline enough to burn eyes and to damage skin on long contact ([Potash](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Potash); [Lye](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lye)). Once you strengthen it with lime, below, it becomes a true caustic in the same class as drain cleaner and must be handled as one. Boil the lye with clean rendered fat, stirring, until the mixture strings or beads off the paddle like jelly. There is no useful fixed ratio, because everything depends on how strong the lye came out: test it by floating a fresh egg or a small potato, which should sit with a coin-sized patch showing above the surface, then make a small test batch and scale up from whatever worked ([Soap making](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Soap_making); [Soap making (CD3WD/VITA)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/SOAPMAKE.HTM)). The fat and the alkali react to make soap and glycerol; that reaction is saponification and it is the whole of the chemistry ([Saponification](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Saponification)).

Ash lye alone gives soft, jelly-like soap. Two improvements: stir in slaked lime to convert the carbonate to a much stronger hydroxide before boiling, and salt the finished soap with common salt to throw out a hard bar ([Soap](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Soap); [Substituting carbonate for hydroxide in soap](kiwix:chemistry.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions/150073/substituting-k2co3-for-koh-in-soap)).

**Lye burns.** It destroys skin and, in the eye, sight, and the burn goes on working after it stops hurting. Wear eye protection and gloves, work outdoors or with the door open and do not lean over the pot or breathe the steam, add the alkali to the liquid rather than the other way round, and flush any splash with running water for **at least 20 minutes** ([Pouring lye and oil](kiwix:chemistry.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions/107591/don-t-pour-oil-into-lye-pour-lye-into-oil)). For an eye splash, hold the lids open and keep flushing while somebody else fetches help: alkali burns go on damaging the eye long after the pain has stopped. Never store lye in a drink bottle and never leave it where a child can reach it.

## Charcoal

Charcoal is wood cooked without enough air to burn it, and it matters because it burns far hotter than wood and without smoke — no forge, lime kiln or pot kiln works properly without it. Either build an earth clamp — a stacked cone of wood sealed under turf with a few controlled vents — or use a retort, a sealed steel drum of wood heated inside an outer fire, which is cleaner and yields better. Under average conditions wood gives about 25% charcoal by weight, or 60% by volume ([Charcoal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Charcoal); [Charcoal (Appropedia)](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Charcoal); [Charcoal making (CD3WD/VITA)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/VITAHTML/SUBLEV/EN1/CHARCOAL.HTM)).

**Burn it outdoors, always.** A smouldering clamp and a bag of cooling charcoal both give off carbon monoxide, which has no smell and kills people who thought they were only keeping warm — never in a shed, garage, tent, caravan or porch, and never bring a barbecue indoors to finish ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide); [Carbon monoxide poisoning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Carbon_monoxide_poisoning)). A clamp burns for days and needs watching: keep water and a rake to hand, well clear of buildings and standing crops. **Let the charcoal cool spread out in the open for a day before you bag it**, because fresh charcoal absorbs oxygen, self-heats and has set fire to the sacks it was put into; it is not reckoned safe until it has been exposed to air for about eight days ([Spontaneous combustion](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Spontaneous_combustion)).

## Lime

Heat limestone, chalk or clean seashells above about 825 °C and the carbon dioxide is driven off, leaving quicklime; working kilns are run nearer 900 to 1,000 °C to get it done in reasonable time ([Calcium oxide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Calcium_oxide); [Lime kiln](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_kiln)). Slake the quicklime by adding it to plenty of water — that way round — and it becomes slaked lime, or lime putty, which improves the longer it is kept under water.

**Quicklime is dangerous in a way that surprises people.** Slaking releases enough heat to boil the water and to ignite nearby combustible material, it spits, and both quicklime and slaked lime are caustic to skin, lungs and eyes ([Calcium oxide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Calcium_oxide); [Quick lime, slaked lime and lime water](kiwix:chemistry.stackexchange.com_en_all/questions/66789/difference-between-quick-lime-slaked-lime-lime-water-and-soda-lime)). Goggles, gloves, long sleeves, outdoors, upwind.

Lime pays for the trouble many times over: mortar and plaster, limewash that both decorates and disinfects, lime for latrines and burials, lime to sweeten sour ground, lime for tanning hides, and lime to strengthen ash lye for soap ([Lime](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_(material))).

## Mortar and plaster

Lime mortar is lime putty and sharp sand, roughly one to three by volume, worked well and used soft. It sets by taking carbon dioxide back out of the air over weeks and months, not by drying, so it must be kept damp and frost-free while it cures: do not lay it below 5 °C, and protect it from frost for about three months. That slowness is the reason old buildings survive: lime mortar is weaker than the stone, so it cracks and repairs rather than splitting the wall ([Lime mortar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_mortar)). The same putty with a little more sand and a handful of animal hair per bucket makes plaster for internal walls and ceilings on riven laths ([Lime plaster](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_plaster); [Small-scale building (CD3WD)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/CD3WD/CONSTRUC/G06SME/INDEX.HTM)).

## Pottery

Clay is under most of Britain: look in river banks, ditch spoil and the subsoil, and test it by rolling a sausage and bending it round a finger without cracking. Wedge the air out, build by pinch, coil or slab, and dry slowly and evenly under a cloth. Firing above about 600 °C, which a simple pit or bonfire firing reaches, makes the change permanent; earthenware is normally biscuit-fired between about 1,000 and 1,150 °C and glaze-fired between about 950 and 1,050 °C, and is fired below 1,200 °C ([Pottery](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pottery); [Earthenware](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Earthenware); [Kiln](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Kiln); [Do-It-Yourself: Pottery](kiwix:wikibooks_en_all_maxi/Do-It-Yourself/Pottery)). Unglazed earthenware is porous, which is a virtue for a water cooler and a nuisance for a storage jar; take glazes seriously only once the kiln is reliable, and never glaze with anything containing lead.

## Glass

Glass is sand, an alkali flux — soda ash or wood potash — and lime as a stabiliser. Silica on its own melts at 1,723 °C, which is out of reach of anything you will build for a long time; the flux is what drops it, and a soda-lime mixture starts to soften from as low as about 700 °C while a real melting furnace works around 1,450 to 1,600 °C, and hotter still at the burners ([Glass](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Glass); [Soda–lime glass](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Soda–lime_glass)). That temperature, held for hours, in a container that will itself survive it, is why glass is a late step and not an early one ([Glassblowing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Glassblowing)).

Meanwhile, salvage: Britain is full of window glass and bottles, and cutting, grinding, slumping and re-melting existing glass all need far less heat than making it from sand.

## Alcohol: fuel, antiseptic, and the law

Yeast turns sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and stops when the strength gets too much for it, so fermentation alone gives wine and beer strengths and nothing stronger ([Fermentation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Fermentation)). Distilling concentrates what fermentation made: ethanol boils at 78.2 °C, and simple distillation cannot get past the ethanol–water azeotrope at 95.6% by mass, about 97% by volume ([Ethanol](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ethanol); [Distillation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Distillation)). Strong alcohol is a fuel for lamps and spirit stoves, a solvent, a preservative and a skin antiseptic — a rub needs at least 60% alcohol to be worth anything, and it does not replace soap and water or sterilise instruments ([Hand sanitizer](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hand_sanitizer); [Bioalcohols as fuel](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Bioalcohols_as_fuel)).

**Methanol.** Methanol causes blindness, organ failure and death, it cannot be smelled or tasted out of a drink, and no amount of discarding the first runnings makes a contaminated or industrial spirit safe ([Methanol toxicity](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Methanol_toxicity)). The reason that rule is about the *source* rather than the technique is that ordinary fermentation and distillation of fruit or grain do not make methanol in dangerous amounts: poisonings come from spirit adulterated with industrial methanol. Never drink spirit of unknown origin, never drink denatured or industrial alcohol, and keep fuel alcohol in a labelled, coloured container that is never used for anything else.

**The law.** In the UK you may brew beer, cider and wine at home in unlimited quantity for your own consumption, but producing alcoholic products needs HMRC approval under section 82 of the Finance (No. 2) Act 2023, which replaced the old Alcoholic Liquor Duties Act 1979 provisions on 1 August 2023. The home-use exemption in section 84 of that Act covers beer, cider and wine but expressly does not cover spirits, so there is no personal-use loophole for distilling, and that remains true whatever your intended use ([Homebrewing: legality](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Homebrewing)).

## Paper and ink

Paper is any cellulose fibre — cotton and linen rags first, then flax, nettle, straw and grass — soaked, beaten to a pulp, floated in a vat, lifted on a mould and deckle, couched onto felt, pressed and dried; size the sheet with gelatine or starch or the ink will feather ([Papermaking](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Papermaking); [Papermaking (Wikibooks)](kiwix:wikibooks_en_all_maxi/Papermaking); [Paper making (Appropedia)](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Paper_Making)). For ink, the two that keep are lamp black ground with gum, which is stable for centuries, and iron gall ink from oak galls, iron sulphate and gum, which was Europe's standard writing ink and is worth knowing is mildly corrosive to the paper it sits on ([India ink](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/India_ink); [Iron gall ink](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Iron_gall_ink)).

## Cloth

Wool: shear, skirt off the dirty edges, wash gently in warm water without agitation, card, then spin on a drop spindle before you build a wheel, and ply two singles together for a yarn that will not twist back ([Wool](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wool); [Spinning wool](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Spinning_wool); [Drop spindle](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Drop_Spindle_for_Spinning_Wool_into_Yarn)). Flax for linen: pull rather than cut, ret it in water or on dewy grass until the woody core lets go, then break, scutch and hackle it before spinning ([Flax](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Flax); [Retting](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Retting); [Spinning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Spinning_(textiles))). A rigid-heddle or simple four-shaft loom will weave everything a household needs ([Loom](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Loom)). Expect the spinning, not the weaving, to be the bottleneck: it always was.

## Salt

Seawater carries around 35 g of salt per litre, so a litre yields about 35 g and it takes some thirty litres to make a kilogram ([Seawater](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seawater)). In a British climate the sun will not finish the job: concentrate the brine in shallow pans, or in winter by letting fresh ice form and lifting it off, then boil it down over a fire — which is why coastal salt-making was always a fuel-hungry trade ([Salt](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Salt); [Salt evaporation pond](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Salt_evaporation_pond)). Inland there are brine springs and the Cheshire rock salt field.

## Preserving

Salting and curing, drying, smoking, pickling in vinegar and fermenting in brine will keep almost everything a British year produces, and none of them needs a jar that seals — though two of them carry their own botulism risk and are worth naming: cured meat needs proper curing salt rather than common salt ([Curing salt](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Curing_salt)), and nothing at all — garlic, herbs, vegetables — should be stored submerged in oil at room temperature ([Food preservation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Food_preservation); [Curing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Curing_(food_preservation)); [Pickling](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pickling); [Preserving food by drying](kiwix:appropedia_en_all/Preserving_Food_by_Drying); [Food processing (CD3WD)](kiwix:cd3wdproject.org_en_all/www.cd3wdproject.org/CD3WD/FOODPROC/M0010E/INDEX.HTM)).

**Canning has a hard limit and it is worth stating plainly.** A sealed jar of low-acid food at room temperature is the ideal home for *Clostridium botulinum*, whose toxin paralyses and kills. Boiling-water processing is only adequate for high-acid foods, meaning below pH 4.6 — most fruit, properly acidified pickles and jams. Low-acid foods, which is vegetables, meat, fish and soups, need a pressure canner, where steam under pressure reaches about 116 °C, aiming at the botulinum cook of 121 °C for three minutes throughout the jar ([Home canning](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Home_canning); [Botulism](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Botulism); [USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning](kiwix:usda-2015_en/home); [Cookbook: Canning](kiwix:wikibooks_en_all_maxi/Cookbook:Canning)). Without a working pressure canner and a tested time and pressure, do not can low-acid food at all: dry it, salt it, ferment it, or keep it cold.
