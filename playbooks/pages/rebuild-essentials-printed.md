This is the printed fallback for the day the box stops. Print the whole page as a bundle, one sheet to a side of A4, before you need it; each sheet stands alone and can be handed to whoever is doing that job ([Keeping the box alive](page:rebuild-keeping-the-box)).

## Fire, three ways

Grade the fuel before you strike anything: a fistful of match-thin tinder, an armful of pencil-thick kindling, then finger and wrist thickness. Take dead standing wood and split thick pieces, because the inside is dry when the outside is soaked ([Fire](page:fieldcraft-fire)).

- **Flame.** Matches or a lighter into a tinder nest; keep two lighters and dry tinder in a tin.
- **Spark.** A ferrocerium rod scraped with a knife spine throws sparks hot enough for fine tinder; flint on high-carbon steel does the same into charred cloth ([Ferrocerium](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ferrocerium)).
- **Friction.** A bow drill: bearing block, bow, spindle and a hearth board of dry softwood, worked until the dust pile forms an ember, tipped into the tinder nest and blown to flame ([Bow drill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Bow_drill)).

Tinder here: birch bark, dead gorse, dead bracken, thistle and willowherb down, fatwood. Light at the bottom, upwind, feed thin sticks fast then slow. **Never burn anything inside a closed room, tent or vehicle: carbon monoxide has no smell and kills while you sleep** ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).

## Water

**Find it.** A spring or fast stream above all grazing, buildings and paths; rain off a clean sheet, which needs no treatment; indoors, the hot water tank and the cistern, never the bowl. Avoid still water, anything below farmland, foaming, smelling or near dead animals ([Water outdoors](page:fieldcraft-water)).

**Settle** until the silt drops and pour off the clear water; **filter** through clean cloth or a bed of sand and gravel, which takes out mud and parasites but not viruses.

**Make it safe**, one of two ways ([Water disinfection](page:water-disinfection)):

- **Boil.** A rolling boil for one minute kills everything, including Cryptosporidium. The surest method.
- **Chlorinate.** 2 drops (0.1 ml) of thin, unscented bleach at 4.5 to 5% sodium hypochlorite per litre, 30 minutes' wait, double both if cloudy or cold. Chlorine does **not** kill Cryptosporidium, so filter first and boil where you can. Never thick or perfumed bleach.

Store treated water covered, cool and dark, use it within a day, and label treated and untreated containers. Water smelling of fuel or chemicals cannot be made safe by anything here: leave it.

## Sanitation

Two rules: keep faeces away from water and food, and wash hands.

- **Outdoors:** a pit latrine at least 30 metres from any well, spring or watercourse and downhill of it, 50 metres from anyone's borehole. Cover each use with soil or ash ([Sanitation module](module:sanitation); [Pit latrine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pit_latrine)).
- **In a house with the sewer dead:** do not flush; what you flush comes back. Use a lidded bucket lined with a bag, urine separate so the solids stay dry. The Chief Medical Officers' advice is to collect faeces in plastic bags, sealed and put out with the household waste, ideally in an outside bin ([UK CMOs, water and sanitation](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-health-advice-from-uk-cmos-during-a-national-power-outage/water-and-sanitation-scripts-for-broadcast-media)).
- **Hands:** soap and water for 20 seconds after the toilet, before food and after handling waste. When water is short, one bowl of water with soap or disinfectant in it serves the household all day; anyone with diarrhoea gets their own. Soap and water beats hand gel ([UK CMOs, water and sanitation](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-health-advice-from-uk-cmos-during-a-national-power-outage/water-and-sanitation-scripts-for-broadcast-media)).
- **Surfaces** fouled by vomit or faeces: one part 5% thin bleach to nine parts water; never on skin.
- **Rubbish:** food waste sealed, buried or burned, never plastics; standing rubbish brings rats, and rats bring leptospirosis.

## First aid

**Severe bleeding.** Press hard with a pad and do not let go for at least 10 minutes. If blood soaks through, add more on top, never removing the first pad. Pack a deep wound in the groin, armpit or neck and press on the packing. For a limb you cannot control, a tourniquet 5 to 7 cm above the wound, not over a joint, tightened until bleeding stops, the time written on it. Lie them down and keep them warm ([Severe bleeding](card:severe-bleeding)).

**Not breathing normally: CPR.** Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, other hand on top, arms straight. Press 5 to 6 cm deep, 100 to 120 times a minute, letting the chest come fully back up. After 30 presses, tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose and give 2 breaths of a second each; if you will not give breaths, keep pressing. Swap every two minutes ([CPR, adult](card:cpr-adult)).

**Unconscious but breathing: recovery position.** Near arm out at a right angle, palm up; far arm across the chest, back of that hand against the near cheek; far knee up, foot flat; pull that knee towards you to roll them onto their side. Tilt the head back and lift the chin. Check breathing every minute, turn to the other side after 30 minutes ([Recovery position](card:recovery-position)).

**Burns.** Cool under cool running water for 20 minutes; cooling still helps up to three hours later. Take off jewellery and loose clothing near it, leave stuck cloth, never use ice, butter or creams. Cover loosely with cling film laid lengthways or a clean non-fluffy cloth, do not burst blisters, keep the rest of them warm ([Burns and scalds](card:burns)).

## Seed saving

Set next year's seed aside before anyone eats this year's harvest. Save from open-pollinated varieties, not F1 hybrids, whose vigour does not carry into the next generation and whose seed does not come true ([Seed saving](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seed_saving)). Save from many plants, and from the best of them, earliest, healthiest, heaviest, because that is how a variety adapts to your ground; keep varieties of one species apart or they cross. Beans, peas, lettuce and tomatoes mostly self-pollinate and are the easy ones; carrots, onions, beetroot and the cabbage family are biennial and seed only in their second year, so some must be overwintered.

Dry seed thoroughly and store it cool, dark and dry in a sealed jar labelled with the variety and year. Ten seeds on a damp cloth in the warm, counted after a week, tell you how thickly to sow ([Growing food module](module:growing-food)).

## Soap and lye

Soap is fat plus alkali, and washing hands with it is one of the cheapest health measures there is ([Hand washing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hand_washing)).

**Lye** was traditionally leached from wood ashes with water, giving a liquor rich in potash; adding slaked lime turns it into stronger caustic potash ([Lye](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lye); [Potash](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Potash)). Pack hardwood ash into a barrel over a straw or gravel bed, pour water through slowly, collect what runs out.

**Soap.** Render clean animal fat, warm it, stir in the lye liquor slowly and keep stirring until it thickens; pour into a mould, cut, and cure it for weeks. Ash lye varies in strength, so find the ratio by small test batches, not by a formula ([Soap](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Soap); [Saponification](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Saponification)).

**Warning: lye burns.** It destroys skin and eyes on contact and can blind, it is harmful or fatal swallowed, and dissolving dry lye in water gives out enough heat to burn or ignite what is nearby ([Lye](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lye)). Work outdoors, cover eyes and hands, keep children and animals away, flood any splash with water.

## Lime and mortar

Lime is mortar, plaster, limewash and a disinfectant for floors and latrines. Burn limestone or chalk in a kiln until it becomes quicklime ([Lime kiln](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_kiln); [Calcium oxide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Calcium_oxide)). Slake it by adding quicklime **to** water, never water to a heap of quicklime: a litre of water takes about 3.1 kg of quicklime and gives out about 3.54 MJ, so it boils, spits and steams ([Calcium oxide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Calcium_oxide)). The result, slaked lime, keeps for months under water.

Mortar and plaster are slaked lime and sand, roughly one to three, mixed stiff. Lime mortar sets slowly by taking carbon dioxide back out of the air, stays flexible and lets a wall breathe, which is why old British buildings use it ([Lime mortar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lime_mortar)).

**Warning:** quicklime burns skin, eyes and lungs, and the heat of slaking can set fire to what is around it ([Calcium oxide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Calcium_oxide)). Goggles, gloves, long sleeves, upwind, outdoors.

## Charcoal

Charcoal is wood cooked with almost no air until only carbon is left. It burns far hotter than wood, which is what a forge, a smelter and a pottery kiln need ([Charcoal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Charcoal)).

**Method.** Either an earth clamp — a dome of split wood covered with turf and earth, lit at the centre, with vents opened and closed to keep it smouldering rather than burning — or, more easily controlled, a sealed steel drum with a small vent, heated by a fire underneath. Both take most of a day and a night and need watching throughout; too much air and the clamp burns to ash ([Charcoal kiln](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Charcoal_kiln)). Let it cool sealed before opening: fresh charcoal reignites in air.

**Warning: never burn charcoal indoors, in a tent, a garage or a vehicle, and never bring a barbecue inside to cook or to warm a room.** It gives off carbon monoxide, which has no smell and kills quickly ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)).

## Units

- **Second.** A pendulum 0.994 m long takes one second per swing at ordinary British gravity ([Seconds pendulum](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seconds_pendulum)). The day is 86,400 of them.
- **Metre.** Originally one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole through Paris ([Metre](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Metre)). In practice, copy a surviving rule or tape onto a seasoned hardwood staff; the 0.994 m pendulum above is the fallback.
- **Litre.** The volume of a cube 10 cm on a side ([Litre](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Litre)); build one in wood or tin and it measures everything else.
- **Kilogram.** Originally the mass of one litre of water, near 4 °C where water is densest ([Kilogram](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Kilogram)). Fill your litre, balance it against a stone, and that stone is your kilogram; an equal-arm balance beats any spring scale you can make.
- **Degree.** Celsius sets 0 at melting ice and 100 at water boiling at sea-level pressure; the melting point barely moves with pressure, the boiling point falls as pressure drops, so calibrate the boiling end where you live ([Celsius](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Celsius)).

## The calendar and the clock

**Noon.** Set a straight stick upright on flat ground and mark the tip of its shadow through the day: the shortest shadow is local apparent noon and points true north here. That line plus the stick is a sundial ([Sundial](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Sundial)).

**Sun time is not clock time.** A sundial runs ahead of mean time by up to 16 minutes 33 seconds around 3 November and behind by up to 14 minutes 6 seconds around 11 February, agreeing near 15 April, 13 June, 1 September and 25 December ([Equation of time](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Equation_of_time)). It matters for navigation, not for farming.

**The year** is 365.24219 days ([Tropical year](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tropical_year)). That quarter-day is why leap days exist: add one every 4 years, skip it in century years unless the year divides by 400 ([Gregorian calendar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Gregorian_calendar); [Leap year](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Leap_year)).

**Keep a day count.** One person marks every day on a board, dated from a day everyone agrees. A village that loses the date loses the sowing calendar and the records with it.
