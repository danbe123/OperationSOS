## The scale this page is written for

A street, a village, a few hundred people: too many for one household's stores and too few to rebuild anything alone. The first month belongs to the playbook for whatever happened; this page is the year after it, when the question changes from surviving the week to feeding and warming everyone through to next harvest ([Total collapse and the long rebuild](playbook:long-rebuild); [Community module](module:community)). Work in the order things kill people here. Shelter first, then water: in cold weather the need for shelter can outrank both food and water ([FM 21-76, p. 38](doc:fm-21-76-survival#page=38)). Then sanitation, then food, then security. Recovery "can last months, years or even decades" and works best when the affected community takes part in deciding how ([NRR 2025, p. 22](doc:nrr-2025#page=22)).

## Water for a few hundred people

One household can carry water in bottles. A village cannot, so the first job is a source a rota can work.

- **Find it.** A spring or well above and away from grazing, buildings and latrines; a fast stream high in its catchment; best of all a borehole with a hand pump, which does not depend on the weather ([Well](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Well); [Hand pump](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hand_pump)). Every private source is untreated water ([Water outdoors](page:fieldcraft-water)).
- **Settle, filter, then disinfect.** A settling tank drops the silt; a slow sand filter does the rest, and is what Victorian cities used: a bed of about a metre of sand under about a metre of standing water, run at 100 to 300 litres per square metre per hour, cleaned by a living biofilm on the sand rather than by the sand itself ([Slow sand filter](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Slow_sand_filter); [Biosand filter](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Biosand_filter)). Slower is better: the cleaning is biological and it needs contact time, and a new filter does not do it at all until the biofilm has had weeks to establish. Then boil or chlorinate: a rolling boil for one minute kills every germ including Cryptosporidium, though it does nothing about chemical contamination, while 2 drops of thin unscented 4.5 to 5% bleach per litre and a 30-minute wait does not, so filter first and double the dose if the water is cloudy ([Water disinfection](page:water-disinfection)).
- **How much.** Three litres a head a day for drinking is the survival floor. The humanitarian minimum once cooking and washing are counted is 15 litres a head a day, and 7.5 litres is a short-term emergency floor rather than a target ([Water module](module:water)); the Chief Medical Officers' outage script asks for at least 2 litres of fluid a day, around 8 cups, and 1 litre for a child under two who is not breastfed ([UK CMOs, water and sanitation](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/public-health-advice-from-uk-cmos-during-a-national-power-outage/water-and-sanitation-scripts-for-broadcast-media)).
- **Protect it.** Latrines at least 30 metres from any well, spring or watercourse and downhill of it, 50 metres from anyone's borehole, with the bottom of the pit at least 1.5 metres above the water table — and further than that on chalk, limestone or any fissured rock, which is most of Britain. No grazing, washing or burial upstream ([Sanitation module](module:sanitation); [Pit latrine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pit_latrine)). One person owns the source. Keep treated and untreated containers apart and labelled.

## Food: what is in stores, fields and byres

Count before you eat: household stores, shops and warehouses, standing crops, roots still in the ground, hay and straw, and every animal within walking distance, on one list ([Food module](module:food); [Food storage](page:food-storage)). Taking goods from a shop or a house is still theft, so agree it with whoever holds them and write down what was taken and what is owed ([Security and the law module](module:security-law); [Theft Act 1968 s1](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/60/section/1)).

Then ration, from the first week rather than from the empty shelf. Britain has done this: rationing began on 8 January 1940 with bacon, butter and sugar, and by August 1942 almost everything except vegetables and bread was rationed; each person held a book and was registered with one shop; the 1918 ration basket — sugar, fats, tea, jam and meat — came to about 1,680 calories on top of bread, which was never rationed, and was adjusted for children, vegetarians and heavy workers; and the population's health did not fall ([Rationing in the United Kingdom](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Rationing_in_the_United_Kingdom)). Copy the shape of it: a weekly allowance per person on a board, everyone registered with one store, extra for children, pregnant and breastfeeding women, the sick and heavy outdoor workers. The base figures are roughly 2,000 kcal a day for a woman and 2,500 for a man at ordinary activity; anyone doing farm or building work all day needs nearer 3,000, which is the number the land arithmetic is built on ([Food module](module:food); [Food storage](page:food-storage); [Farming for a decade](page:rebuild-farming)).

Grow calories, not salad. A traditional 10-rod allotment, about 250 square metres, was reckoned to keep a family of four in vegetables for a year — vegetables, not staple calories, which need the acres on [Farming for a decade](page:rebuild-farming). Potatoes give more food energy per acre than any cereal, roughly 2.2 kg per square metre at world average yields ([Growing food module](module:growing-food); [Potato](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Potato)). Set next year's seed aside before anyone eats this year's crop, keep breeding stock alive whatever the hunger, and plan for the hungry gap of April to early June ([Hungry gap](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hungry_gap); [Livestock module](module:livestock)). The rotation, fertility without bought inputs and the seed bank belong to the decade after this year ([Farming for a decade](page:rebuild-farming)). Get a grain crop and a mill going early: a hand quern feeds a household, a watermill a village ([Quern-stone](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Quern-stone); [Gristmill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Gristmill)). Store the surplus by drying, salting, smoking and a cool cellar or clamp, and keep rats and weevils out ([Root cellar](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Root_cellar); [Salting](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Salting_(food)); [Butchery](page:butchery)).

## Fuel and warmth for a winter

Firewood is the year's second harvest and it is cut a year before it is burned. Coppice woodland is cut in coups on a rotation so a crop comes off some part of the wood every year and the stools live for centuries ([Coppicing](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Coppicing)); seasoning matters because unseasoned wood gives less heat and tars a flue ([Wood drying](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wood_drying); [Firewood](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Firewood)). Sweep chimneys, because a chimney fire in a village of thatch and timber is not one family's problem ([Chimney fire](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Chimney_fire)).

Heat one room per household rather than a house, insulate it, and share fires where you can: one fire and one pot feed a dozen households on less fuel than a dozen kitchens ([Shelter and heat module](module:shelter-heat)). Nothing that burns fuel goes in a closed room without a flue and ventilation, and charcoal is never burned indoors: carbon monoxide has no smell and kills people who thought they had opened the window enough ([Carbon monoxide card](card:carbon-monoxide)). For a fixed appliance, the flue sizing, air supply and alarm rules are in [Approved Document J](doc:ad-j).

## The register: who can do what

On paper, in the hall, where anyone may read it: every person, their age, what they can do and what they need. Nurses, midwives, vets, farmers, growers, butchers, blacksmiths, plumbers, electricians, engineers, radio amateurs, teachers, beekeepers, anyone who has kept animals or built anything. Then the assets: tools, generators, chainsaws, vehicles, fuel, animals, land, seed, medicines, halls and wells. Then the people who need care ([Community module](module:community); [Ready Scotland community planning](kiwix:govuk_resilience/ready.campaign.gov.scot/community-emergency-planning)).

One rule makes the register worth keeping: every critical skill has a second person who can do it, taught before they are needed. The year you lose the only person who can set a bone is the year the village changes.

## The rota

Write the week down and pin it up. Water and the filter; latrines and refuse; wood cut, split and stacked; animals fed and milked; the growing ground; cooking; nursing the sick; the watch; the children's lessons; the record book. Everyone has a job, including children and the very old, because a role is what keeps a community whole ([Community module](module:community)). Name a rest day. Rotate the jobs nobody wants rather than giving them to whoever complains least, and put the names on the board so the work is visible.

## The charter: a few rules, written down

Half a page, agreed at a meeting, dated, signed, and nailed where everyone passes it. Six or seven rules is plenty: fair shares and what the ration is; that the ledger is open to anyone; who decides what, and how they are chosen; how a dispute is heard; that work earns a share and refusal to work is a matter for the meeting, not for a fight; what happens to strangers who arrive; and how the charter itself is changed. Minute every meeting. A dispute in year three is settled by the book written in week one, and the detail of rules, disputes, land and records is on [Law, records and trade](page:rebuild-law-and-trade).

## Deaths, the coroner and burial

People will die, and the record you make is the only certificate until an office reopens. Confirm the death with two people, write down the name, the date, time and place, who was present, what happened and the cause as best you know it, and keep the body cool and labelled ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief)). A death that is sudden, violent, in custody or of unknown cause is a coroner's matter in England, Wales and Northern Ireland and a procurator fiscal's in Scotland, and it stays one however long the office takes to reopen; register the death whenever a registrar can be reached ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death); [Coroner](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Coroner)).

Burial without an undertaker is lawful in England and Wales, and no statute forbids burying a body on private land, but the landowner must agree, the Environment Agency's distances from water are binding, planning and the deeds may constrain it, the death must be registered when it can be, the grave must be recorded, and the council's environmental health department should be told as soon as one can be reached; moving a body afterwards needs a licence, not just a spade ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death); [Natural burial](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Natural_burial)).

Site the burial ground once, and site it by the Environment Agency's numbers rather than by eye. Its guidance on protecting groundwater from human burials, and its conditions for low environmental risk cemeteries, set the grave **at least 250 metres from any well, borehole or spring used to supply drinking water or food production, at least 30 metres from any spring or watercourse, at least 10 metres from any field drain or dry ditch, and with at least a metre of dry ground between the bottom of the grave and the top of the water table** — never uphill of a source, and never on land where water stands. That guidance is not in this box's library, so the figures are recorded here: they are an order of magnitude larger than the 30-metre latrine rule above, and a grave sited by the latrine rule can be eight times too close to a well ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief); [Natural burial](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Natural_burial)). One grave per person, marked so it can be found in a year, a sealed note of the name in with the body and the location sketched in the village register: mass graves lose identities, and missing certificates cause families years of trouble ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief)). Bodies of people who died of injury do not start epidemics; a grave in the wrong place near water does ([Sanitation module](module:sanitation)). Someone who died of cholera, dysentery or a haemorrhagic fever is a different matter: gloves, hands washed afterwards, the body wrapped and closed, and buried well away from water ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief)). Hold a funeral of some kind, however small: it matters more than it seems ([Mental health module](module:mental-health)).

## The first winter

The first winter is the test, and it is won in August. By the equinox you want: firewood counted in weeks of burning and already drying, the food store counted in days and written on the door, the ration set, seed and breeding stock ring-fenced, one warm room per household ready, the water source working in frost, latrines dug before the ground hardens, and the register of who gets checked on twice a day ([Shelter and heat module](module:shelter-heat); [Community module](module:community)).

Then keep the discipline that hunger and cold erode: hands washed, water treated, latrines used, the sick nursed apart, the ledger written up ([Sanitation module](module:sanitation)). Cold kills the old, the very young and the ill first, so the twice-a-day check is not kindness, it is the rota ([Hypothermia card](card:hypothermia)). Expect the hungry gap from April into early June, not in January, and hold back enough to get through it.
