## The numbers

UK mains supply is 230 V single phase at 50 Hz. BS 1363 plugs each carry their own fuse, usually 3 A for lamps and small appliances and 13 A for anything that draws more current, such as a kettle or heater.

## The consumer unit

The consumer unit holds a main switch, 30 mA residual current devices (RCDs) that trip on an earth fault fast enough to prevent electrocution, and miniature circuit breakers (MCBs) sized to the circuit: typically 6 A for lighting, 16 or 20 A for radial sockets, 32 A for a ring final circuit wired in 2.5 mm² cable, and 32 or 40 A for a cooker or electric shower. Test the RCD's test button every three months ([HSE INDG231, p. 3](doc:hse-indg231#page=3)). UK homes use one of three earthing arrangements — TN-S, TN-C-S or TT — which is why a generator needs its own separate earth rather than borrowing the house supply's.

## What you may and may not do

Under Part P of the Building Regulations, new circuits, a new or replacement consumer unit, and most electrical work in a bathroom are notifiable and must be carried out or checked by a registered electrician ([Approved Document P, p. 7](doc:ad-p#page=7)). Before touching any wiring: isolate the circuit at the consumer unit, then prove it is dead with a proper voltage tester — never work on anything live. Run extension leads unwound, not daisy-chained together. Water and electricity do not mix: never touch switches, sockets or appliances with wet hands, and keep portable heaters and lamps well away from flooded areas.

## Generators and batteries

A generator or battery system must only feed the house wiring through a proper transfer switch that disconnects the property from the grid first; connecting one directly to a socket can send power back down the street cables and kill a lineman working on what they believe is a dead network ([Power module](module:power)).
