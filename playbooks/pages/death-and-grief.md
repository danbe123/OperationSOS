## Confirming death

Never treat anyone as dead until you and another person agree that breathing has stopped (listen with your ear over the nose and mouth, watch the chest, hold a mirror to the lips), that the heart has stopped (no pulse at the neck, no heart sounds with your ear on the left chest), and that the eyes are dull with large pupils that do not shrink in a bright light ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 1](doc:scmg-ch12#page=1)). Check, leave the body alone, and check again after 30 minutes to be sure ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)). Two mistakes are made again and again: the very cold person and the person who has taken a large overdose of sedatives can look dead and still be alive, so "you are not dead until you are warm and dead"; in the cold, keep warming and keep checking before deciding ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 1](doc:scmg-ch12#page=1); [Hypothermia card](card:hypothermia)). The eyelids do not close on their own after death; someone closes them ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)).

## If nobody can come

{{#if phones}}For a sudden, unexpected or violent death, [[call 999]]: the police attend and the coroner takes over. For an expected death at home, ring the GP surgery or [[call 111]], and then the funeral director. {{else}}With the phones down, a death is not an emergency for the person who has died. Send a runner to the police station for a sudden or violent death and to the surgery or a funeral director for an expected one, when someone can be spared ([getting help without phones](page:no-phones)). {{/if}}While you wait, do the things a coroner will later need. Write down the name, the date and time, who was present, what happened in the hours before, your own guess at the cause, and what you did with the body; photograph the scene and the body if the death was sudden or unexplained; strip and list clothing and possessions, bag anything wet, seal, label and keep them; do not launder anything ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 2](doc:scmg-ch12#page=2); [Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 104](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=104)). If the circumstances are unusual, sudden, unknown or suspicious, leave the scene as it is and touch as little as possible ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 2](doc:scmg-ch12#page=2)).

## Care of the body

Wear gloves and wash your hands afterwards; that is all the hygiene a body needs unless the person died of an infectious disease, in which case take the precautions you would have taken while nursing them ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)). The bodies of people killed by injury or by most illnesses do not start epidemics; the one real risk is a body or a grave contaminating a water supply, which causes stomach illness ([health risks from dead bodies](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Health_risks_from_dead_bodies)).

Within the first hours, before stiffness sets in: wash and dry the body, comb the hair, close the eyes and mouth (a bandage under the chin and over the head holds the jaw), straighten the arms and legs, interlock the fingers over the thighs, tie the ankles together, and press firmly on the lower belly to empty the bladder ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 3](doc:scmg-ch12#page=3)). Rigor mortis begins about two hours after death and is complete by around eighteen hours, then passes off after a day or two ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)). Wrap the body in a sheet or a body bag, label it on the outside with the name and the date and time of death, and put a second label inside ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 2](doc:scmg-ch12#page=2)).

Cooling: the aim is about 4 °C, not frozen ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 3](doc:scmg-ch12#page=3)). A cold room with the heating off and the window open, a garage or an outbuilding in winter, or ice packed around the body in a bath all serve; in hot weather a body can begin to smell within eight hours, in cool weather 24 to 36 hours, and a frozen body does not decompose at all ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)). Keep the body away from food and from the living space, and keep children and pets out of the room.

## Registering the death and the coroner

In England and Wales a doctor or the medical examiner's office confirms the cause, and the death is then registered at the register office within 5 days; in Scotland it is 8 days ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death)). The registrar issues the certificate for burial or cremation, "the green form", which goes to the funeral director, cemetery or crematorium before the funeral can happen ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death)). A death that is sudden, unexpected, violent, in custody or of unknown cause is reported to the coroner, who may order a post-mortem and an inquest and who releases the body when the examinations are done; the paperwork for registering then comes from the coroner, and an interim certificate is available while an inquest runs ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death); [coroner](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Coroner)). Scotland has no coroners: such deaths go to the procurator fiscal ([coroner](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Coroner)). When no registrar can be reached for weeks, the record you made above is what the registrar and the coroner will work from later; keep it with the household papers ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 104](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=104)).

## Temporary burial

Burial at home is a last resort for when the body cannot be kept cool and nobody can come for days. Register the death first if there is any way to do so, and record everything as above whether or not you can ([After a death](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/after-a-death); [Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 104](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=104)). Environment Agency guidance for England sets minimum distances between a grave and any well, borehole or spring used for drinking water, and larger ones than the sanitation rule below; that guidance is not in this box's library, so treat the figures here as the floor, tell the council's environmental health department as soon as it can be reached, and expect the body to be moved to a cemetery later. As the floor, apply the latrine rule: at least 30 metres from any well, spring or watercourse and downhill of it ([Pit latrine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pit_latrine)), on ground where water does not stand, deep enough that animals cannot reach the body and away from any field drain. Wrap and label the body as above, put a sealed note of the name and date in with it, mark the grave with something that will survive a winter, and write the exact location, with a sketch, into the household record ([Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 2](doc:scmg-ch12#page=2)). One grave per person: mass graves demoralise survivors and lose identities, and the missing death certificates cause years of trouble for the family ([health risks from dead bodies](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Health_risks_from_dead_bodies)). Most faiths have short rites for burial; for the non-religious a favourite poem or piece of music does the same work, and it matters more than it seems ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 103](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=103)).

## Grief

What is normal: emptiness, exhaustion, confusion, anger, guilt, even relief; tightness in the throat or chest, breathlessness, an upset stomach, sleeplessness. Symptoms may come at once or be delayed until some task is finished, usually last weeks to a few months, and settle with time and company ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 390](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=390); [Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 395](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=395); [Grief](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Grief)). Most people are resilient and carry on functioning through a loss ([Grief](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Grief)). Do the things the [Mental health module](module:mental-health) lists: talk at your own pace to someone you trust, accept practical help, keep routines, and do not drink through it.

When to worry: grief that is still severe and daily after six months, with the person unable to resume ordinary life, "giving up", or physical illness, is prolonged grief and needs help ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 395](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=395); [prolonged grief disorder](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Prolonged_grief_disorder)). Any talk of suicide, at any stage, is acted on today: ask directly, remove the means, stay with them, and use the helplines when the lines work ([Mental health module](module:mental-health); [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)). Bereavement raises the risk of illness and of suicide, especially in teenagers who have lost a parent ([Grief](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Grief)).

Children: tell them the truth in plain words ("died", not "gone to sleep"), let them ask the same question many times, keep them with familiar adults and in their routines, and let them join in what the family does for the person who died. Young children show less distress than adults and work through what happened in play, and a child under about a year is distressed by the separation itself; both need presence, not explanation ([PTSD](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder); [Grief](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Grief)). Childline and the other numbers are on [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers).
