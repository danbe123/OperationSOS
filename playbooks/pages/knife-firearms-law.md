## Knives

**Can I carry a penknife?** Yes, if the blade is folding, non-locking and 3 inches (7.62 cm) or less. Anything locking, fixed, or longer than that needs "good reason" to carry in public, such as work or a specific planned activity; "self-defence" is not a good reason and never has been ([CJA 1988 s139](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/33/section/139); [GOV.UK knives](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/buying-carrying-knives)). Scotland's equivalent offence is in the Criminal Law (Consolidation) (Scotland) Act 1995 s49 ([s49](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1995/39/section/49)).

## Prohibited weapons

**What can I not own even at home?** A list of weapons is banned outright under section 141 of the 1988 Act and the Offensive Weapons Act 2019, including zombie-style knives and machetes (banned from 24 September 2024), ninja swords (banned from 1 August 2025), knuckledusters, batons and disguised knives such as those hidden in a belt or a phone case ([CJA 1988 s141](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/33/section/141); [Offensive Weapons Act 2019](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/17/contents); [GOV.UK knives](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/buying-carrying-knives)). Carrying any article made or adapted for causing injury, or carried with that intent, is an offensive weapon under the Prevention of Crime Act 1953 ([Prevention of Crime Act 1953](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/1-2/14)).

**Pepper spray or a stun gun?** Both are prohibited weapons under section 5(1)(b) of the Firearms Act 1968, "any weapon of whatever description designed or adapted for the discharge of any noxious liquid, gas or other thing"; the courts have held that stun guns fall under the same paragraph (Flack v Baldry, 1988), and CS, Mace and pepper sprays are named in the Home Office guidance to the police ([Firearms Act 1968 s5](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/27/section/5); [firearms licensing guide](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/firearms-law-guidance-to-the-police-2012/guide-on-firearms-licensing-law-accessible-version)). Possession is an offence whatever your intent and is dealt with in the Crown Court. The mandatory minimum sentence that Parliament attached to prohibited firearms applies to the gun categories in section 5 (subsection (1)(a) to (af) and (c), and (1A)(a)), not to the sprays and stun guns in subsection (1)(b), but a prison sentence is still the normal outcome ([Firearms regulation in the United Kingdom](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Firearms_regulation_in_the_United_Kingdom)).

## Air weapons, shotguns and rifles

**Air rifles?** In England and Wales no certificate is needed for an air rifle up to 12 foot-pounds or an air pistol up to 6 foot-pounds of muzzle energy; above that they are "specially dangerous" and need a firearm certificate ([firearms licensing guide, para 2.46](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/firearms-law-guidance-to-the-police-2012/guide-on-firearms-licensing-law-accessible-version)). You must be 18 to buy or possess one, and it is an offence to fire one beyond the boundary of the premises ([Firearms regulation in the United Kingdom](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Firearms_regulation_in_the_United_Kingdom)). Scotland requires an air weapon certificate from Police Scotland for any air weapon ([Air Weapons and Licensing (Scotland) Act 2015](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2015/10/contents)). Northern Ireland treats any air weapon above 1 joule as a firearm needing a certificate ([Air gun laws](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Air_gun_laws)). **Shotguns and rifles?** Both need a certificate issued by the police before you can own one ([Firearms Act 1968 s1](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/27/section/1); [firearms licensing](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/guidance/firearms-licensing-police-guidance)).

## Crossbows, catapults and bows

**Crossbows** need no licence, but the Crossbows Act 1987 makes it an offence to sell or hire one to anyone under 18, for anyone under 18 to buy or hire one, or to possess one unless supervised by someone aged 21 or over ([Crossbows Act 1987 s1](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1987/32/section/1); [contents](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1987/32/contents); [Crossbows Act 1987](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Crossbows_Act_1987)). As at September 2026 the government has been legislating on crossbows through the 2025 to 2026 Crime and Policing legislation; check for licensing or age changes before relying on this page. **Catapults and bows** have no licence and no age limit; there is no law specific to them, but one carried "for protection" is an offensive weapon under the 1953 Act ([Prevention of Crime Act 1953](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/1-2/14)). Using any of them on wild animals is prohibited by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981, which makes it an offence to kill or injure most wild birds and many wild animals without a specific exemption ([WCA 1981 s1](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1981/69/section/1); [Foraging, fishing and hunting law](page:foraging-law)).

## Defending yourself and your home

The law allows such force as is reasonable in the circumstances to prevent crime and to make a lawful arrest ([Criminal Law Act 1967 s3](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1967/58/section/3)); defending yourself or another person comes from the common law, and defending property is narrower still — force is not lawful against a trespasser who is committing no crime. Section 76 of the Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 says how a court judges that: on the facts as you honestly believed them, allowing that a person under attack cannot weigh things to a nicety. In a "householder case", confronting an intruder inside your own home, grossly disproportionate force is never lawful and force short of that must still be reasonable — a higher bar than for other self-defence, but not a licence, and it covers defending people inside the dwelling rather than property or the garden ([CJIA 2008 s76](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2008/4/section/76); [self-defence in English law](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Self-defence_in_English_law)). You may not set traps or booby-traps, and keeping a weapon specifically to use against an intruder undermines any claim that the force was reasonable.

{{#if scenario:civil-unrest}}
**Nothing here is suspended by a riot.** Carrying a knife, a bat or a spray "for protection" makes it an offensive weapon under the Prevention of Crime Act 1953, and carrying a blade out of a general fear of attack is not a good reason in law: that defence very rarely succeeds ([Prevention of Crime Act 1953](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/1-2/14); [CJA 1988 s139](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/33/section/139)). At home the law allows such force as is reasonable in the circumstances as you honestly believed them to be, and for a householder facing an intruder grossly disproportionate force is never lawful while force short of that must still be reasonable ([CJIA 2008 s76](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2008/4/section/76)). Lock up, stay in, and do not go out armed ([Security and the law module](module:security-law)).

## Scotland and Northern Ireland

Section 76 extends to England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and refers to both the English and the Northern Irish Criminal Law Acts of 1967; the householder subsections (5A) and (8A) to (8F), however, were inserted for England and Wales only, so in Northern Ireland the test is ordinary reasonable force under section 3 of the Criminal Law Act (Northern Ireland) 1967 ([CJIA 2008 s76, extent and textual amendments](kiwix:legislation_uk/www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2008/4/section/76)). Scotland is outside section 76 altogether: self-defence there is common law, and the same reasonable-force principle applies without the householder extension. Northern Ireland has its own firearms law, the Firearms (Northern Ireland) Order 2004, administered by the Police Service of Northern Ireland, and the Great Britain exemptions do not apply there ([firearms licensing guide](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/government/publications/firearms-law-guidance-to-the-police-2012/guide-on-firearms-licensing-law-accessible-version)).
