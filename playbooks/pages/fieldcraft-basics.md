## What kills people outdoors in Britain

Not starvation, not wild animals, not thirst. Wet cold. Britain is a mild country with weather that changes in an hour, and most people who die on hills, moors and coasts here die of exposure, falls in poor visibility, or cold water. A soaked person in a summer wind at 12°C can be hypothermic in a few hours; at 5°C with rain it can be one ([Hypothermia](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Hypothermia); [Wind chill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Wind_chill)). Every page in this set follows from that fact.

The order of priorities is the same everywhere but the weights are British:

1. **Stop and think.** Panic, and walking on in the dark or the mist, cause most accidents. Sit down, eat something, get out of the wind, and decide.
2. **Shelter and warmth**: from wind and rain first, then from the ground ([Shelter and staying warm](page:fieldcraft-shelter)).
3. **Signal and be found**: the phone, the whistle, the route you told someone ([Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue)).
4. **Water**: you have a day or two, more in cold weather, and Britain is rarely short of it ([Water outdoors](page:fieldcraft-water)).
5. **Food**: you have weeks. Foraging is a skill for the long rebuild, not for the first night ([Wild food](page:fieldcraft-food)).

## Weather is the enemy and the timetable

Fronts arrive from the south-west, so a falling barometer, a wind backing from west to south, and high thin cloud thickening from the west all mean rain within hours. On high ground the temperature drops roughly 1°C for every 100 m of climb, and the wind at 600 m can be double the wind in the valley ([Reading the weather and exposure](page:fieldcraft-weather)). Plan every move with a turn-back time, and turn back when you reach it.

## The law is part of field craft here

- **Access.** In England and Wales you may walk public rights of way and open access land under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000; that is not a right to camp, light fires or take anything ([CROW Act](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Countryside_and_Rights_of_Way_Act_2000)). Scotland's right of responsible access covers most land and allows wild camping and small fires with care ([Scottish Outdoor Access Code](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scottish_Outdoor_Access_Code)). Wild camping in England is lawful only on parts of Dartmoor and where a landowner agrees, though in a real emergency nobody will prosecute someone sheltering for a night.
- **Fires.** Open fires on moorland, heath and most access land are banned, and in dry spells whole counties can be closed to them. Use a stove wherever you can ([Fire in a wet country](page:fieldcraft-fire)).
- **Taking things.** Picking fruit, fungi, flowers and foliage for your own use is lawful under the Theft Act; uprooting plants is not; game, deer, fish and most animals are protected or licensed ([Foraging and the law](page:foraging-law); [Knife and firearms law](page:knife-firearms-law)).
- **Knives.** A folding blade under three inches is the everyday limit; a fixed blade needs a good reason, and being in the woods is one only while you are actually using it ([Knife and firearms law](page:knife-firearms-law)).

Law matters less once society has stopped functioning, but the habits it builds (small fires, no trace, nothing uprooted) are exactly the habits that keep a landscape feeding you for years.

## Rescue in Britain

There is no 911 and no forest ranger. The number is **999**, or **112** — [[call 999]]. Ask for the **police** for anything inland, including mountain rescue, which the police call out; ask for the **coastguard** for anything on the coast, cliffs, estuaries or in the sea; ask for the **ambulance** for a medical emergency you can reach by road. Mountain rescue teams are volunteers and free, and they would far rather be called early than find you the next morning ([Mountain rescue in England and Wales](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Mountain_rescue_in_England_and_Wales); [HM Coastguard](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/HM_Coastguard)). If the phones are down, the rules change: see [Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue) and the [comms module](module:comms).

## Kit that earns its place

A British day-sack holds, before anything else: a waterproof jacket and overtrousers, a warm hat and gloves even in July, a spare warm layer in a dry bag, a head torch with spare cells, a whistle, a map and compass and the skill to use them ([Navigation module](module:navigation)), water, more food than you expect to eat, a small first aid kit ([Medical module](module:medical)), a foil blanket or better a group shelter, a lighter and a knife within the law. A phone is a bonus, not a plan.
