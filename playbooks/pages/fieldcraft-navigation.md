## The map you have

The box's map shows an Ordnance Survey grid reference for the centre of the view and for any point you tap, and the same grid is printed on every OS paper map. A reference is two letters for the 100 km square and then a string of figures, always eastings (across, left to right) before northings (up): six figures name a 100 m square, eight a 10 m square ([OS National Grid](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ordnance_Survey_National_Grid)). To read one off a paper map, take the number of the grid line to the left of the point, estimate tenths of the square across to the point, then the grid line below and tenths up; "right and up" is the whole rule ([FM 3-25.26, p. 36](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=36)). The summit of Ben Nevis is NN 166 712 ([OS National Grid](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ordnance_Survey_National_Grid)). Ireland, north and south, uses its own Irish Grid, which reads the same way ([Irish grid reference system](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Irish_grid_reference_system)). Read a grid reference out loud in pairs, letters first, and have the listener read it back: a six-figure reference is what the police and mountain rescue want from you ([Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue)).

{{#unless power}}
This box is running on a battery like everything else, so read what you need and write it on paper before the screen goes: your grid reference, the reference of where you are going, the bearing between them, and the tide times if you are near the coast ([Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue)). A paper Ordnance Survey map and a baseplate compass need no charge at all and are the primary tools here; the phone and this box are the backup. Keep the spare cells for the head torch out of the cold, and the compass away from all of it.
{{/unless}}

The brown contour lines are the shape of the ground; the closer they are, the steeper the slope. Learn to see the map as hills and valleys before you need to, because in mist the contours are the only thing you can check against ([Contour line](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Contour_line)).

## Taking a bearing and walking it

A baseplate compass is the tool: a needle, a rotating housing marked in degrees with an orienting arrow and lines in its floor, and a direction-of-travel arrow on the plate ([Compass](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Compass)).

1. **From the map**: lay the long edge of the plate from where you are to where you want to go, direction-of-travel arrow pointing the way. Ignore the needle. Turn the housing until its floor lines run parallel to the map's north-south grid lines with the orienting arrow pointing to the top of the map. Read the bearing at the index line ([FM 3-25.26, p. 62](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=62)).
2. **Add the variation**: magnetic north and grid north are not quite the same, and the difference is printed in the map margin. In Britain it is now small: at London it was one degree west in 2014 and reached zero in 2020, and everywhere in the country it is within a degree or two of zero, so for walking it can be ignored, but check the margin of an old map, whose printed value may be several degrees out of date ([Magnetic declination](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Magnetic_declination); [FM 3-25.26, p. 62](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=62)).
3. **On the ground**: hold the compass flat in front of you, turn your whole body until the red end of the needle sits inside the orienting arrow, and the direction-of-travel arrow now points the way. Pick a **steering mark** on that line, a rock, a tree, a change in the vegetation, as far away as you can see, walk to it without looking at the compass, then take the next. Never choose a mark from the map; choose it from what you can see, and at night choose a bolder shape ([FM 3-25.26, p. 138](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=138)). Keep the compass away from the phone, a knife, a torch and a car, which all pull the needle.
4. **Back bearings**: the bearing back to where you came from is the bearing plus 180 if it is under 180, minus 180 if it is over; 60 becomes 240, 300 becomes 120. Use it to check you are still on the line by sighting back on the mark you left, to return along a leg, and to get round an obstacle: a mistake in the sum is the classic way to walk confidently the wrong way ([FM 3-25.26, p. 58](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=58)). A quicker check than arithmetic: sight back with the white end of the needle in the orienting arrow instead of the red.

To go round something you cannot cross, turn 90 degrees, pace a known distance, turn back to the bearing, pass the obstacle, turn 90 the other way, pace the same distance, and resume ([FM 3-25.26, p. 100](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=100)).

## Staying on track without a straight line

Dead reckoning by compass and pace alone drifts, and the errors add up over distance, so borrow the ground's help ([FM 3-25.26, p. 140](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=140)):

- **Aiming off**: if the target sits on a line you must hit anyway, a stream, a wall, a track, a road, aim deliberately to one side of it by a few degrees, so that when you reach the line you know which way to turn. Each degree of offset moves you about 18 m to the side for every kilometre walked, and about ten degrees is enough ([FM 3-25.26, p. 100](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=100)).
- **Handrails**: a wall, a stream, a ridge, a forest edge, a power line or a track running your way is a rail to follow without precise compass work; hold it on your left or right until it turns away from your route.
- **Catching features**: pick something that tells you when you have gone too far, the stream beyond the target, the road at the bottom of the slope, so an overshoot is caught early.
- **Attack points**: navigate fast and roughly to a big obvious feature near the target, a stream junction, a bridge, a wall corner, a summit cairn, then go carefully on a bearing and a pace count for the last stretch; keep that last stretch short, ideally under 500 m ([FM 3-25.26, p. 140](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=140)).

Each time you know exactly where you are, on a cairn, at a junction, at a bridge, start the pace count and the timing again from there ([FM 3-25.26, p. 139](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=139)).

## Pacing and timing

**Pace counting** turns your legs into a tape measure. Measure your own count on a known 100 m, a marked athletics straight, a length taped or paced on a road, and count every double step (every time the same foot lands); a course over ground like the ground you will walk gives a truer figure than a pavement. Then keep the count honestly: a pebble moved from one pocket to another, a knot in a cord, or a string of pace-counting beads slid across, once every hundred metres, because nobody can remember it ([FM 3-25.26, p. 53](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=53); [Pace count beads](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pace_count_beads)). The count lengthens going uphill and shortens going down, and is shortened by wind in the face, mud, snow, heavy boots, fog, rain and darkness; a night pace is short enough to deserve its own measured figure ([FM 3-25.26, p. 53](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=53); [FM 3-25.26, p. 141](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=141)). In a group, one person keeps the compass and a different person keeps the pace, and neither is the one out in front watching the ground ([Ranger Handbook, p. 131](doc:tc-3-21-76-ranger-handbook#page=131)).

**Timing** is the other measure. Naismith's rule: one hour for every 5 km on the flat plus one hour for every 600 m of climb, which is one minute for every 10 m of ascent, for a fit walker in ordinary conditions ([Naismith's rule](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Naismith's_rule)). For a short leg in mist, work it out before you start: a 500 m leg climbing 50 m is six minutes of walking plus five of climbing, so if eleven minutes have passed and the wall is not there, stop and think rather than walk on hoping. Time and pace together catch most errors; a watch that runs is part of the navigation kit.

{{#if dark}}
At night the steering mark has to be a bold shape you can still make out, a skyline notch, a wall end, a light, and the pace count is the short night figure you measured, not the day one ([FM 3-25.26, p. 138](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=138); [FM 3-25.26, p. 141](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=141)). Keep the compass away from the head torch, which pulls the needle like any other torch, though no source gives a reliable distance; aim off deliberately so you know which way to turn when you meet a wall or a stream, and shorten every leg. Polaris gives north to within about a degree when the compass is lost ([Polaris](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Polaris)).
{{/if}}

{{#if scenario:severe-winter}}
Snow takes the handrails away: paths, walls, streams and the shape of the ground disappear, cornices hang over edges that look like the top of the slope, and a whiteout removes the horizon ([Severe winter](playbook:severe-winter)). Navigate on bearing and pace count alone, one person on the compass and another counting, with short legs and a catching feature behind every target ([FM 3-25.26, p. 139](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=139); [Ranger Handbook, p. 131](doc:tc-3-21-76-ranger-handbook#page=131)). Your stride shortens in deep snow, so the count that gave 100 m on a road will not give it here.
{{/if}}

## Finding north without a compass

- **The sun**: it rises roughly in the east, is due south at local noon, and sets roughly in the west; in the British summer, with the clocks an hour ahead, it is due south at about one o'clock ([British Summer Time](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/British_Summer_Time)). In midsummer it rises and sets well to the north of east and west, in midwinter well to the south, so "rises in the east" is only roughly true here.
- **An analogue watch**: point the hour hand at the sun; south lies halfway between the hour hand and twelve o'clock, or between the hour hand and one o'clock while the clocks are on summer time. Before noon the sun is in the east and after noon in the west, which settles which end of the line is north ([FM 3-25.26, p. 102](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=102)). A digital watch works too: draw the time as a clock face on paper or in the mud. The method is rough, within a few degrees at best, and rougher in summer and near dawn and dusk.
- **The shadow stick**: push a stick upright into level ground and mark the tip of its shadow with a stone. Wait ten or fifteen minutes and mark the new tip. The line from the first mark to the second runs west to east, first mark west; stand with the first mark on your left and you face north. It works in bright cloud if the shadow is visible at all, and with a bright moon at night ([FM 3-25.26, p. 101](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=101); [FM 3-25.26, p. 141](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=141)).
- **The Pole Star**: find the Plough (the Big Dipper), the seven-star saucepan that is always above the British horizon; the two stars at the end of the pan away from the handle are the Pointers, and a line through them, carried on five times their spacing, reaches Polaris, a middling star on its own at the end of the Little Bear's handle. Polaris is less than a degree from true north, and its height above the horizon is your latitude, roughly halfway up the sky in Britain ([FM 3-25.26, p. 103](doc:fm-3-25-26-map-reading#page=103); [Polaris](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Polaris)). Cassiopeia, the W on the other side of Polaris, finds it when the Plough is low.

None of these replaces a compass; a compass costs little, weighs nothing and works in cloud. Carry one, and a spare in the group.

## Tides

British tides are large, they come in through channels that fill behind you, and the box carries no tide tables because the official data is not openly licensed. So learn to reason about the tide from three things you do have.

- **The clock**: on most British coasts there are two high waters a day, about 12 hours 25 minutes apart, so each day's high water is about 50 minutes later than the day before; if you saw high water yesterday you know roughly when it is today ([Tide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tide)). The **rule of twelfths** says how the water moves between them: in the six hours from low to high (or high to low) the level changes by one twelfth of the range in the first hour, two in the second, three in the third, three in the fourth, two in the fifth and one in the sixth. Half the whole rise happens in the middle two hours, which is why sands that were dry an hour ago are suddenly under fast water ([Rule of twelfths](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Rule_of_twelfths); [Tide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tide)).
- **The moon**: the biggest tides, **springs**, come around new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line and their pulls add; the smallest, **neaps**, come around the first and last quarter. The cycle repeats about every two weeks ([Tide](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Tide); [Lunar phase](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lunar_phase)). The box's **Sun and moon** tool (Tools, then Sun and moon) shows today's phase, so you know whether you are on springs, when the water comes furthest in, drops furthest out and runs fastest, or neaps, when it does less of everything. On springs the high water can reach places that were dry for the last week, and the low water uncovers sands that are a trap.
- **The strand line**: the line of weed, driftwood and rubbish left along a beach marks how far the last high tide reached, and a second, higher line of older dry debris marks the last springs ([Beach wrack](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Beach_wrack); [High water mark](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/High_water_mark)). Camp above the highest line, not the fresh one. Wet sand, ripples still holding water, and weed lying pointing one way tell you the water left recently and is coming back.

The rule is simple: **never cross sands, an estuary, a causeway or the foot of a cliff without a time you trust for the next high water and a route off that stays open**. If the time came from yesterday's observation and the rule of thumb, add a wide margin and turn back at it. Morecambe Bay, the Wash, the Solway and the Severn all have taken people who knew the sands ([Morecambe Bay](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Morecambe_Bay)). If the water cuts you off, do not wade channels: climb to the highest ground, signal, and ask for the **coastguard** — [[call 999]] ([Getting found](page:fieldcraft-rescue); [Moving across country](page:fieldcraft-moving)).
