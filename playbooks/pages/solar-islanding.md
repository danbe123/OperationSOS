## Why the panels go dark

Grid-tied solar inverters are required to disconnect the moment the grid fails, a safety feature called loss-of-mains protection under the G98 and G99 connection rules, so that linesmen repairing the network are not electrocuted by power fed back from someone's roof ([Islanding](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Islanding); [Solar inverter](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Solar_inverter)). The result is that a standard grid-tied array, however sunny the day, produces nothing usable the moment the power cuts out.

## Keeping some power

There are two ways round it. A hybrid inverter paired with a battery and an emergency power supply (EPS) output can automatically island a separate circuit when the grid drops, feeding chosen sockets or a consumer unit sub-board; a manual changeover switch does the same job by hand. Both routes involve notifiable electrical work under Part P and need a registered installer ([Approved Document P, p. 9](doc:ad-p#page=9)). The simpler alternative is a portable "solar generator": a lithium power station of around 1 kWh paired with a folding 100 to 200 W panel, enough to run a fridge for about a day, or keep phones and radios charged for weeks.

## What a panel gives in Britain

UK solar yield varies hugely by season: a 1 kWp array gives roughly 0.5 to 1 kWh a day in December and 4 to 5 kWh a day in June, so a small 100 W panel in midwinter is realistically a phone charger, not a heater. A car battery, reached through its 12 V socket and a 150 W inverter, is a useful fallback when there is no sun ([Vehicles and fuel](module:vehicles-fuel)). LiFePO4 batteries, the type used in most solar generators, hold about 1,280 Wh per 100 Ah at 12.8 V and tolerate several thousand charge cycles ([Lithium iron phosphate battery](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lithium_iron_phosphate_battery)).
