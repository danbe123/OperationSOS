## Why this page matters more than the fire

In every camp, shelter and refugee column in history, more people have died of diarrhoea than of cold or violence. A group living outside a working sewer for a week will meet norovirus, campylobacter, giardia and cryptosporidium unless it keeps its water, its hands and its latrine apart ([Norovirus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Norovirus); [Sanitation module](module:sanitation)). The rules are simple and boring, and they only work if everyone follows them every time.

## The latrine

- **At least 50 metres** from any water, any shelter and any food, downhill and downwind, and not where water will run through it.
- A **cat hole** for one person for one night: 15 to 20 cm deep, filled and covered. A **trench latrine** for a group staying longer: a spade's width, 60 cm deep, earth kept beside it and a shovelful thrown in after each use, a lid or a board over it, moved when it is 30 cm from full.
- Toilet paper burned or buried; sanitary products and wipes carried out or burned, never buried, because animals dig them up.
- In a town without working sewers, a bucket with a bin liner and sawdust or soil, emptied into a deep pit well away from wells, is the standard; the [sanitation module](module:sanitation) covers the household version.
- On sensitive ground (peat, sand dunes, caves, above the treeline) everything is carried out.

## Hands

Wash hands with soap after the latrine and before touching food, every time. Where water is short, a "tippy tap": a bottle hung from a stick with a string to tilt it, so the group washes from a litre. Alcohol gel works against bacteria and most viruses but not against norovirus or cryptosporidium; soap and water do. Nails short; one person handles the food.

## Food and animals

Cook thoroughly, eat straight away, and do not keep cooked food overnight without a fridge. Rats, mice, foxes and crows find any camp within a night; hang food from a branch in a bag or box, keep the cooking area away from the sleeping area, and bury or burn scraps far from camp. Rat urine on stored food and in still water spreads leptospirosis (Weil's disease), which starts as flu and can kill; cover water containers and wash the tops of tins before opening them ([Leptospirosis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Leptospirosis)).

## Skin, feet and ticks

Dry socks every night and feet checked daily prevent trench foot, the wet-cold injury that ends more British expeditions than frostbite ([Trench foot](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Trench_foot)). Wash the body, especially groin, armpits and feet, once a day if there is any water at all; a cloth and half a litre is enough. Check the whole body for ticks each evening from March to October in bracken, heather and long grass, and remove them at once with tweezers pulling straight out; watch the bite for a spreading ring over the following month ([Ticks and adders](page:ticks-adders); [Lyme disease](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lyme_disease)). Treat blisters early with a plaster over clean skin, and small cuts by washing them; in field conditions an infected cut becomes a real problem within days ([Wound cleaning card](card:wound-cleaning)).

## Washing kit and clothes

Wet kit dries in wind, not in a tent; a line under the tarp edge and a fire at a distance. Sleeping bags aired every dry morning. Boots stuffed with dry grass or paper overnight. Clothes washed in a bucket with soap, rinsed well away from streams, because even biodegradable soap poisons the small life in cold water.

## When someone is ill

Diarrhoea and vomiting: isolate them with their own latrine and bowl, the rest of the group washes hands more, and the patient drinks oral rehydration salts, a litre for every large loose stool, made up with treated water ([Dehydration card](card:dehydration)). Most bouts pass in two or three days; blood, fever over 39°C, or a child or old person who cannot keep fluids down needs medical help ([Medical module](module:medical)).

## Leaving

Fill the latrine, scatter the fire site cold, pick up every scrap of plastic and foil, and leave the place so that the next family, or you next year, can use it. The Countryside Code is good manners in normal times and good sense in bad ones.
