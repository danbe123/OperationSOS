## What it is

Operation SOS is an offline knowledge box for a UK household in an emergency: one small device on its own WiFi, with no internet needed, that holds what you need to get through the first hour, the first week and the years after. It works from the moment it is switched on. Nothing has to be typed in first, no account, no register; the only thing it ever asks is how many people it is feeding, so the kits can scale.

## How to use it

The front door asks one question: **what's the situation?** Twenty situations are laid out as tiles, from a power cut to a nuclear strike to the long rebuild after everything stops, and under them a row of services: power, water, mobile, landline, internet, gas, heating, roads, shops, sewage. Tap a service to say it is off, and again when it is back. That is all the box needs to start working for you.

From then on the box follows the situation. The to-do list on the Situation sheet and on the board is written for the scenario you chose, in the order things should be done, and it changes as services fail or return. The guides change too: a medical card read while the phones are down carries the plan for nobody coming; the water guide read in a flood says what the flood does to the water; a passage that appears because of your situation is labelled with why. In peacetime you read the general page; in a situation you read the page for that situation.

The rest of the box, in the order you will probably need it:

- **Medical**: thirty-seven quick cards for the first minutes (bleeding, CPR, choking, burns, stroke and the rest), the NHS website saved offline, and a medical library behind them.
- **Map**: the country's roads, footpaths and terrain, with layers for hospitals, fuel, water, rail, flood zones, access land, nuclear and chemical sites, airports and military land. Tap a place and the box tells you what it usually has, when it is worth going, when to stay away and how to go about it, with the distance and walking time from your home or from wherever the map is. A blue dot marks where the device says you are.
- **Kits**: tick-lists of what to have, in three tiers from a long weekend to three months, scaled to your household, and a tab that lists everything you have marked.
- **Guides**: the topic modules (water, food, shelter and heat, power, communications, security and the law, navigation, community and more), the UK field-craft pages, the reference pages, and a **Rebuilding** section for the years after: the first year at village scale, keeping this box alive, a primer the box prints out one sheet a side, restarting science and measurement, making things again, iron and tools, power from scratch, medicine without industry, farming for a decade, law and records, and a map of what to read in the library and in what order.
- **Find** searches everything on the box at once. The assistant, when switched on, answers from the library and shows what it quoted.
- **The board** turns the kiosk into a screen the room can read: what is off, the next jobs, sunset, the next radio bulletin. Services and jobs can be worked from it.

## Where the content comes from, and how it was checked

Every item in the library shows an "as at" date and a licence. The NHS pages are a dated snapshot under the Open Government Licence v3. Map data comes from OpenStreetMap under the ODbL, flood zones from the Environment Agency, Natural Resources Wales, SEPA and the Office of Public Works, and open access land from Natural England and Natural Resources Wales. The science library (OpenStax and LibreTexts) is on the box under Creative Commons licences. The playbooks, cards, pages and the map guidance were written from the sources cited under each one, and every citation opens the source it was drawn from ([National Risk Register 2025](doc:nrr-2025); [Prepare](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/about-this-website-and-uk-resilience/)).

Every claim on the map cards and every situational passage in the guides was then checked, one by one, against primary UK sources (the NHS, the Resuscitation Council, the Environment Agency, UKHSA, HSE, the Food Standards Agency, the Acts themselves) and corrected where it was wrong. The reports are kept with the code. That does not make the box infallible; it means the box's numbers came from somewhere you can look up.

## What it is not

This box is not a medical device and does not give medical advice: it shows official pages and first-aid steps, and it tells you plainly when to [[call 999]] or [[call 111]] instead, and what to do when those will not connect. The assistant only quotes from the library and can still be wrong; the library pages are always the source of truth, not its summary of them. The box is not affiliated with the NHS or with any government department. It does no tracking, needs no account, and nothing on it leaves the box: even the saved websites are blocked from calling out.

## Keeping it current, and keeping it alive

The owner runs an update over an ethernet cable when one is available, and the System screen shows when each item was last refreshed. Emergency Alerts, weather warnings and flood warnings are live services provided by others and this box cannot replace them ([Emergency Alerts](kiwix:govuk_resilience/www.gov.uk/alerts)). In a power cut the box runs from a mains adapter or the optional power bank for roughly 8 to 15 hours depending on use ([What still works](page:what-still-works)); how to keep it running for years, and what to print before it dies, is in [Keeping the box alive](page:rebuild-keeping-the-box).
