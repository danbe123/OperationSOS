## What is actually worth restarting

Not the equipment. Glassware, meters and reagents all come back eventually, and the box carries the textbooks that describe them. What is easy to lose in one generation is the habit of mind: asking a question that has an answer, testing it in a way that could show you were wrong, writing down what happened whether or not it suited you, and letting someone else repeat it. Every useful thing on the rest of these pages — a fertiliser trial, a water filter, a hardening temperature, a dose — was found that way and can be found that way again ([Scientific method](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scientific_method)).

## The method, in four moves

1. **Ask something answerable.** Not "is comfrey good for the soil" but "does a barrow of comfrey mulch on this bed give more potatoes than no mulch on that bed".
2. **Compare like with like.** Run the two side by side, in the same season, on the same ground, tended the same way. The untreated half is the control, and without it you have nothing to compare against ([Scientific control](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Scientific_control)). Decide which bed gets which by drawing lots, not by picking, or you will unconsciously give the treatment the better ground ([Randomised controlled trial](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Randomized_controlled_trial)).
3. **Write it down as it happens.** Before you know the result, record what you did, how much, when and what you measured with.
4. **Repeat it, and let someone else repeat it.** One result is an anecdote. Three, on different ground, by different hands, is knowledge.

Where people are involved, add two more: whoever assesses the outcome should not know which person got the treatment, and neither should the person, because both will find what they expect ([Blinded experiment](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Blinded_experiment); [Placebo](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Placebo)).

## Why the stories will mislead you

Most people got better anyway. Most illnesses end. Most seasons are good or bad for reasons nothing to do with what you did. A remedy given to twenty people who would have recovered regardless produces twenty testimonials and no evidence, and the person who gave it will believe in it for the rest of their life ([Anecdotal evidence](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Anecdotal_evidence)). We also remember the hits and forget the misses, and read the record to fit what we already think ([Confirmation bias](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Confirmation_bias)). This is not a reason to ignore what the old and the experienced say — they are the best source of hypotheses you have — it is a reason to test the ones that matter before betting a harvest or a life on them.

## The log

Keep one bound book with numbered pages, written in ink, and never on loose sheets. Each entry: the date, who did it, what was done in enough detail that a stranger could copy it, what was measured and with which instrument, what happened, and what went wrong. Record failures in the same detail as successes; they are more useful and nobody else will write them down ([Lab notebook](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Lab_notebook)). Once a year, copy the pages that matter into a second book kept in another building. Keep a separate running record of the weather, the sowing and harvest dates, births and deaths, and the state of the wells: in ten years that ordinary book will answer questions nobody has thought to ask yet.

## A second, from a pendulum

A pendulum is the one accurate instrument you can build with string and a stone. For small swings its period depends only on its length and on gravity:

**T = 2π √(L / g)**, with T in seconds, L in metres and g about 9.81 m/s² in Britain ([Pendulum](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pendulum); [Standard gravity](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Standard_gravity)).

Turn it round to get the length for a two-second beat: L = g (T / 2π)² = 9.81 × (2 ÷ 6.2832)² = 9.81 × 0.1013 = **0.994 m**. That is the seconds pendulum, one second per swing in each direction, and it is what the good clocks were built around from the 1680s onwards ([Seconds pendulum](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seconds_pendulum)).

To build one: a dense small bob on a thin cord, hung from a firm point, swinging no more than a few degrees, because the formula only holds for small swings. Adjust the length until it beats seconds, and set it by counting a hundred swings against noon-to-noon rather than one against a guess: a hundred swings should take 100 seconds, and any error you make in the count is divided by a hundred. Once you have a seconds pendulum you have a clock, a metronome for anything that must be timed, and a way to check every other clock you ever build.

## The metre, the litre and the kilogram

The pendulum gives you length for free. Its length is 0.994 m, so **1 metre = pendulum length ÷ 0.994**, and you can mark that off on a bar. Be honest about the weakness: gravity varies with latitude and altitude, so a seconds pendulum is a slightly different length in Cornwall and in the Cairngorms, which is exactly why the French rejected the idea in 1790 and measured the Earth instead ([Seconds pendulum](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Seconds_pendulum); [History of the metre](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/History_of_the_metre)). So use the pendulum to get started, then transfer the length to a physical standard — a bar of brass or well-seasoned hardwood, marked at both ends, kept flat, dry and at an even temperature — and thereafter copy the bar, not the pendulum. Better still, find a surviving steel rule or tape and copy that; it was made to a standard nobody now has to re-derive ([Metre](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Metre)).

From the metre, everything else. A cube 100 mm on the inside holds one litre. Fill it with clean water at its coldest useful point — water is densest at about 4 °C — and that water weighs one kilogram, because the kilogram was originally defined in 1795 as the mass of a cubic decimetre of water ([Litre](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Litre); [Kilogram](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Kilogram); [Properties of water](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Properties_of_water)). Make the box carefully, in something that does not swell, and you have re-created two base units in an afternoon.

## Temperature

Two fixed points define a scale. Melting ice, stirred as a slurry of ice and water, is 0 °C; water boiling at sea level is 100 °C, and the boiling point falls with altitude and low pressure, so take it low down on a settled day ([Celsius](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Celsius); [Properties of water](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Properties_of_water)). Mark the liquid level at both points on a sealed tube and divide the space between into a hundred.

Use a **spirit thermometer**, dyed alcohol in glass. Mercury is a cumulative poison, mercury-in-glass thermometers have long been withdrawn from general sale, and a broken one contaminates a room, so do not make or keep them ([Mercury-in-glass thermometer](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Mercury-in-glass_thermometer)). The honest limit of spirit is that ethanol boils at 78.2 °C, so an alcohol thermometer cannot take the boiling-water point at all ([Alcohol thermometer](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Alcohol_thermometer); [Ethanol](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Ethanol)). Graduate it from the ice point and from a second cold fixed point — a saturated slurry of common salt, ice and water settles at about −21 °C — and use it for weather, fever, food, brewing and fermentation, which is most of what you need a number for ([Freezing-point depression](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Freezing-point_depression)). Forge heat is read by colour instead, which is on [Iron and tools](page:rebuild-iron-and-tools).

## A balance and a set of weights

An equal-arm beam balance on a knife edge beats any spring, because it compares masses directly and does not care what gravity is. Build it symmetrical, hang the pans from the same kind of pivot, and check it by swapping the pans: a true balance reads the same both ways. Make the weight set by division rather than by addition — start with the litre of water at one kilogram, split that into two equal masses of lead or stone by balancing them against each other, split again for 250 g, and so on down ([Weighing scale](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Weighing_scale)). Keep one master set sealed away and use working copies, and re-check the copies against the master every year.

## The sky: noon, a sundial and the sextant

Drive a straight stick vertically into level ground and mark the tip of its shadow through the middle of the day. The shortest shadow is local apparent noon and it points along the true north–south line — which also gives you true north without a compass ([Sundial](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Sundial); [Navigation](page:fieldcraft-navigation)).

Sundial noon is not clock noon. The sun runs ahead of mean time by as much as 16 min 33 s around 3 November and behind it by as much as 14 min 6 s around 11 February, a difference called the equation of time; and every degree you stand west of the meridian your clock keeps puts local noon four minutes later ([Equation of time](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Equation_of_time)). Measure the difference across a year with your pendulum clock and you will have re-derived the table yourself.

For latitude, measure the angle of the pole star above the horizon: that angle is your latitude. Polaris is not exactly at the pole — it circles it in a ring about 1.3° across — so a single sight is good to a little over half a degree, which is around 60 km, and averaging sights taken twelve hours apart cancels most of it ([Polaris](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Polaris); [Celestial navigation](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Celestial_navigation)). A salvaged sextant is worth more than its weight in anything; failing that, a quadrant of marked board with a plumb line and two sights will get you within a degree ([Sextant](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Sextant)). Longitude needs an accurate clock carried from a known place, which is a harder problem and worth understanding before you need it.

## The maths worth keeping

- **Arithmetic and fractions**, done on paper and checked, plus a written multiplication table for teaching ([Prealgebra 2e](doc:openstax-prealgebra-2e)).
- **Ratio and proportion**: scaling a recipe, a mortar mix, a dose, a gear ratio or a seed rate is all the same operation.
- **Geometry for building**: a 3-4-5 triangle squares a corner, Pythagoras gives you diagonals and rafter lengths, similar triangles measure the height of a tree or the width of a river from the ground, and areas and volumes give you yields and tank sizes ([Pythagorean theorem](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Pythagorean_theorem)).
- **The statistics of a fair trial**: what a control is, why you allocate at random, why small differences in small groups are usually noise, and what it means to say a difference could easily have arisen by chance ([Statistical significance](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Statistical_significance); [Sample size](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Sample_size_determination); [Introductory Statistics 2e](doc:openstax-introductory-statistics-2e)).

The physics and chemistry behind the units, the instruments and the measurements are on the box in full: [College Physics 2e](doc:openstax-college-physics-2e) for mechanics, heat and electricity, and [Chemistry 2e](doc:openstax-chemistry-2e) for density, solutions and reactions. They assume no equipment you cannot eventually rebuild.
