## The rule for every long-term condition

Very few chronic conditions become dangerous the day treatment stops, but two do: insulin-dependent diabetes and lung disease that needs oxygen. For everything else there are days or weeks to find a supply or a work-around, so learn your own condition now, keep a written list of every medicine and dose, and stock what you can ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 105](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=105)). Every playbook plans for two weeks of every prescribed medicine, and the time to ask the GP or pharmacist about a longer supply and about "sick day rules" is before the emergency ([Medical module](module:medical)). Never share prescription medicines, and never buy them from unregulated sellers.

## Insulin and diabetes

Type 1 diabetes needs insulin to survive; many people with type 2 can manage for months on diet, exercise and tablets if insulin runs out ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 467](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=467); [WTIND, p. 176](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=176)). Keep the supply the NHS advises, enough for one or two months with at least one spare pen, cartridge or vial ([NHS insulin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/insulin/)).

Storage without a fridge:

- Unopened insulin lives in the fridge, never touching the freezer section; insulin that has frozen is thrown away ([NHS insulin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/insulin/)).
- A pen or cartridge in use is kept at room temperature for up to four weeks (28 days for rapid-acting insulin), out of direct sunlight and away from radiators; after that it may not work properly ([NHS insulin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/insulin/)). Write the date it came out of the fridge on the box.
- With the power off, unopened stock goes in the coolest place in the house: a cellar, a north-facing cupboard, an evaporative cooling pouch (Frio and similar, which cools when the crystals are soaked in water), or a sealed bag in a bucket of cold water changed daily. Cold, not frozen: a cool bag with an ice block against the insulin can freeze it ([NHS insulin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/insulin/)). Insulin is one of the few medicines that loses potency without refrigeration ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 46](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=46)).

Emergencies: a hypo is on the [low blood sugar card](card:low-blood-sugar). The opposite danger, ketoacidosis, builds over about a day with vomiting, extreme thirst, passing lots of urine, belly pain and then deep gasping breathing, and is an emergency — [[call 999]] ([diabetic ketoacidosis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Diabetic_ketoacidosis)). Someone on gliclazide who skips meals or drinks alcohol on an empty stomach is at risk of a hypo; regular meals including breakfast prevent it ([NHS gliclazide](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/gliclazide/)). Someone on metformin who is vomiting or has diarrhoea takes small frequent sips to avoid dehydration and asks a pharmacist about the signs; metformin is not stopped without advice, because blood sugar climbs when it is ([NHS metformin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/metformin/)).

## Steroids and adrenal crisis

Anyone with adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease, or a pituitary problem) or on long-term steroid tablets cannot make the cortisol that illness demands. They carry a red steroid emergency card, and a sudden drop in cortisol is an adrenal crisis, a medical emergency ([NHS hydrocortisone](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/hydrocortisone-tablets/)). Prevention is the person's own sick day rules, which raise the tablet dose during fever, infection or injury, and an intramuscular hydrocortisone injection kept at home for when they cannot keep tablets down; gastroenteritis is the classic trigger, and someone in crisis can deteriorate within a few hours ([adrenal crisis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Adrenal_crisis)). Signs are weakness, vomiting, belly pain, dizziness, confusion and collapse: give the injection they carry if they cannot swallow tablets, and [[call 999]] ([adrenal crisis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Adrenal_crisis)). The same applies to anyone whose long-term prednisolone runs out: after months of steroids the adrenal glands have shrunk, and stopping abruptly, or falling ill after stopping, can bring on the same crisis, so a supply runs down gradually, never to zero overnight ([adrenal insufficiency](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Adrenal_insufficiency)).

## Epilepsy medicines

Never stop epilepsy tablets abruptly. Lamotrigine and levetiracetam stopped suddenly can cause seizures, and coming off them properly takes months ([NHS lamotrigine](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/lamotrigine/); [NHS levetiracetam](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/levetiracetam/)); sodium valproate is not stopped without a doctor either ([NHS sodium valproate](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/sodium-valproate/)). Sudden withdrawal of an anticonvulsant is a recognised cause of status epilepticus, the seizure that does not stop ([status epilepticus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Status_epilepticus)). If the supply is finite, the person keeps taking the full dose while a replacement is sought, and only if none can be found reduces it as slowly as the tablets allow. Phenobarbital and phenytoin are the cheap, widely stocked alternatives a pharmacy may still hold ([WTIND, p. 227](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=227)). A fit lasting more than five minutes, or repeated fits, is an emergency — [[call 999]] ([Seizures card](card:seizures)).

## Thyroid

Levothyroxine is lifelong and the symptoms return if it stops, but it is slow: the drug has a half-life of about seven and a half days, so a missed week is not a crisis ([NHS levothyroxine](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/levothyroxine/); [levothyroxine](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Levothyroxine)). A missed dose is taken when remembered, never doubled ([NHS levothyroxine](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/levothyroxine/)). The danger of a long gap is myxoedema coma, rare but fatal, usually set off by another illness: a person with known hypothyroidism who is cold without shivering, confused, with a slow pulse and slow breathing is an emergency — [[call 999]] ([hypothyroidism](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Hypothyroidism)).

## Asthma and COPD

The brown or other preventer inhaler is inhaled corticosteroid; taken every day it suppresses attacks, and it is the one to protect when supplies are short, while the blue reliever (salbutamol) treats an attack ([asthma](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Asthma)). Keep smoke, dust and cooking fires out of the house, and drink plenty ([WTIND, p. 216](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=216)). An attack that ten puffs do not settle is on the [asthma attack card](card:asthma-attack). Oxygen dependence is covered below.

## Anticoagulants

Warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban and edoxaban are not stopped without advice: with apixaban the clot risk is back to what it was within a day or two, and stroke, heart attack, deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism can follow ([NHS apixaban](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/apixaban/)). Warfarin stays active for several days after the last dose and is reversed in hospital with vitamin K ([warfarin](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Warfarin)). A missed dose is taken when remembered unless the next is due, and never doubled ([NHS warfarin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/warfarin/)). On warfarin, keep the amount of vitamin K foods (green leafy vegetables, liver, egg yolks) steady from week to week rather than cutting them out ([NHS warfarin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/warfarin/)). Serious bleeding, a nosebleed that will not stop after ten minutes, blood in vomit or coughed up, means stop the warfarin and get help — [[call 999]] ([NHS warfarin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/warfarin/); [Nosebleed card](card:nosebleed)). Any blow to the head on an anticoagulant is treated as serious ([Head injury card](card:head-injury)).

## Contraception when supplies run out

The NHS contraception pages are not in this box's library, so the figures here are from WikiMed. Condoms are the easiest method to stock: 2% of couples a year get pregnant with perfect use and 18% with typical use ([condom](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Condom)); latex condoms are damaged by oil-based lubricants. The combined pill fails in under 1% a year with perfect use and about 9% with missed doses; a pill more than 24 hours late counts as missed, and another method is needed until seven active pills in a row have been taken ([combined pill](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Combined_oral_contraceptive_pill)). Fertility awareness is only as good as its method: the symptothermal method (temperature and mucus) is about 2% a year in typical use, while the calendar rhythm method alone fails 24% of the time ([fertility awareness](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Fertility_awareness)). A mother who is fully breastfeeding, whose periods have not returned and whose baby is under six months old has under 2% risk with perfect use of the lactational amenorrhoea method ([lactational amenorrhoea](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Lactational_amenorrhea)). Emergency contraception: a levonorgestrel pill within 3 days, ulipristal within 5 days, or a copper coil fitted within 5 days, sooner being better in every case ([emergency contraception](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Emergency_contraception)). Anyone on sodium valproate or warfarin who may be pregnant needs advice urgently ([NHS sodium valproate](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/sodium-valproate/); [NHS warfarin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/warfarin/)); bleeding or pain in pregnancy is on the [pregnancy emergencies card](card:pregnancy-emergencies).

## Alcohol, opioid and benzodiazepine dependence

Taper, never stop. For anyone who drinks heavily every day, withdrawal starts about six hours after the last drink; seizures peak at 24 to 36 hours and delirium tremens at 48 to 72 hours, and delirium tremens kills without treatment ([alcohol withdrawal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Alcohol_withdrawal_syndrome)). The risk of death is about 2% with treatment and around 25% without, so shaking, confusion, hallucinations, fever and a racing heart in a heavy drinker who has stopped is a 999 emergency, not a hangover — [[call 999]] ([delirium tremens](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Delirium_tremens)). When the supply is ending, the safe course is to cut down steadily over days rather than stop, eat, and take thiamine (vitamin B1) if you hold it. If nobody can come, the treatment is a benzodiazepine such as chlordiazepoxide or diazepam on a fixed reducing schedule over several days with someone watching, which is exactly what a hospital would do ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 390](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=390); [delirium tremens](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Delirium_tremens)).

Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam and their relatives) taken for more than a few weeks are reduced gradually; stopping dead brings anxiety, insomnia, tremor and, in some, seizures ([benzodiazepine withdrawal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Benzodiazepine_withdrawal_syndrome); [NHS diazepam](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/diazepam/)).

Opioid withdrawal (heroin, methadone, buprenorphine, strong painkillers) is miserable but not in itself fatal; the dangers are dehydration from vomiting and diarrhoea ([Dehydration card](card:dehydration)) and, later, overdose when someone whose tolerance has dropped uses their old dose ([opioid withdrawal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Opioid_withdrawal)). Methadone is never mixed with codeine or dihydrocodeine painkillers or with sleeping tablets, which raise the overdose risk ([NHS methadone](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/methadone/)). Keep naloxone where it can be found. Antidepressants such as sertraline are also tapered over weeks rather than stopped, or withdrawal symptoms follow ([NHS sertraline](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/sertraline/)).

## Dialysis and oxygen dependence

Dialysis: haemodialysis is normally three sessions a week of three to four hours each, and a person cannot survive long without it ([haemodialysis](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Hemodialysis)). If the unit or the transport fails, the whole household's job is to buy time: keep the renal diet strictly, restrict fluid, and avoid high-potassium foods, because potassium builds up and causes dangerous heart rhythms ([kidney failure](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Kidney_failure)). Anyone on dialysis or home oxygen should be on the Priority Services Register with their electricity and water companies, and the unit or the ambulance service will move patients to whichever unit has power ([UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)); with the phones down, send a runner to the unit itself ([getting help without phones](page:no-phones)).

Oxygen: home oxygen comes from cylinders or an electric concentrator ([oxygen therapy](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Oxygen_therapy)). A concentrator dies with the mains, so plan a battery or generator supply and how long it lasts ([Power module](module:power)), and keep the cylinders for when it is off; a concentrator delivers 5 to 10 litres a minute ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 94](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=94)). Never smoke or bring a naked flame near oxygen; it feeds fire ferociously ([oxygen therapy](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Oxygen_therapy)). For someone with COPD, the target on a pulse oximeter is 88 to 92% if they retain carbon dioxide and 94 to 98% otherwise, so ration oxygen to the target rather than running it flat out ([COPD](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Chronic_obstructive_pulmonary_disease)).

## Other daily medicines

Lithium levels rise with dehydration, so illness, heat or diarrhoea on lithium needs fluids and advice ([NHS lithium](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/lithium/); [Dehydration card](card:dehydration)). Heart and blood-pressure tablets are kept going; if they must be rationed, ask which matter most before the emergency, not during it ([Medical module](module:medical)).

## Medicines that keep and medicines that do not

| Medicine | Keeps? | What the sources say |
|---|---|---|
| Most tablets and capsules (paracetamol, blood-pressure, epilepsy, thyroid) | Yes | Cool, dark and dry; most are safe for 24 to 36 months past the expiry date but slowly lose potency ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 45](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=45)) |
| Antibiotics, dry tablets or capsules | Yes | Safe for years past expiry under the US shelf-life programme when kept cool, dry and dark ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 215](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=215)) |
| Penicillins, dry form | Yes | Usable for a year past the date if kept clean, dry and cool; old stock may need a larger dose ([WTIND, p. 381](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=381)) |
| Tetracyclines (doxycycline) | Caution | The old warning was that degraded tetracycline is toxic; the cause has been removed from modern tablets, but expired stock is still the one class to discard ([WTIND, p. 381](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=381); [Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 46](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=46)) |
| Insulin | No | Fridge until opened, four weeks at room temperature in use, never frozen ([NHS insulin](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/insulin/)); loses potency unrefrigerated ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 46](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=46)) |
| Ergometrine, oxytocin, some muscle relaxants | No | Need refrigeration ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 46](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=46)) |
| Diazepam | Light-sensitive | Loses potency quickly in light; keep in the dark ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 46](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=46)) |
| Home-made rehydration drink | Hours | Cereal-based drink spoils in a few hours in hot weather; taste it each time and make it fresh ([WTIND, p. 201](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=201)) |

The WHO essential medicines list is the reference for which forms of insulin and other long-term medicines a community pharmacy should hold ([WHO EML, p. 54](doc:who-eml-2025#page=54)).
