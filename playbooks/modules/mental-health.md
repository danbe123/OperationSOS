## Key facts

- Psychological first aid is practical help, not therapy: make people safe and comfortable, give them the chance to talk without pressing them to relive the event, find out what they need, help with the practical, and connect them to family, neighbours and services. Formal "debriefing" sessions that make people go over the event are avoided because they have been linked to more post-traumatic stress, not less ([psychological first aid](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Psychological_first_aid); [Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)).
- Feelings that persist beyond two to four weeks are the point to see a GP or self-refer to NHS Talking Therapies ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)); distress that lasts more than a month and stops normal life may be post-traumatic stress disorder, which needs treatment, not just time ([PTSD](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder)).
- Alcohol makes every difficult reaction worse, not better, and anyone who drinks heavily every day must not stop suddenly either: sudden withdrawal can kill ([chronic conditions](page:chronic-conditions); [alcohol withdrawal](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Alcohol_withdrawal_syndrome)).

## What to do

1. In the first hours, meet the basics: warmth, food, a safe place, and honest information about what is happening and what comes next. Sit with anyone who is shaking, crying or silent; do not push them to talk, and do not leave them alone ([psychological first aid](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Psychological_first_aid)).
2. Over the first days, do what the government's own advice lists: share your feelings with someone you trust, at your own pace; be willing to listen to others; ask family, neighbours or a faith community for emotional and practical support; spend some time each day on something you enjoy; and return to everyday routines and habits, because they are comforting and make people feel less out of sorts ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)).
3. With children, give honest, simple answers, keep them with familiar adults, and let them play and help rather than sit and wait: young children show less distress than adults but work through what they saw in play ([PTSD](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder); [Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)).
4. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts if you are worried about someone, remove any means of harm, and do not leave that person alone ([Samaritans](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Samaritans_(charity))).
5. {{#if phones}}Use the helplines: Samaritans 116 123 (free, 24 hours), Shout by texting 85258, NHS urgent mental health help — [[call 111]] and choose the mental health option, in England, Scotland and Wales, with Northern Ireland using its GP out-of-hours service — CALM 0800 58 58 58, which is open 5pm to midnight every day rather than around the clock, and Childline 0800 1111 ([UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)).{{else}}No helpline will answer while the phones are down; the numbers for when the lines return are on [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers), and the routes that do not need a line are on [getting help without phones](page:no-phones).{{/if}} Beside every one of those numbers stands the help that needs no line at all, and it is always available: sit with the person, stay the night if that is what it takes, share the load with another adult, and walk them to a rest centre, a surgery or a church where people are gathering.
6. Watch the calendar. Sleep loss, jumpiness and low mood in the first fortnight are normal; the same things at four weeks, or anyone who has stopped eating, washing or speaking, means a GP or Talking Therapies referral now ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/); [PTSD](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder)).

{{#if scenario:grid-collapse}}
**Dark evenings, no news and rumour are the strain in a blackout.** Keep the shape of the day: meals at the usual times, everyone up and dressed, children in bed at the same hour, one lamp lit where people sit together. Take one radio bulletin a day rather than listening all evening, and answer children in short true sentences, "I do not know yet" included. Give everyone a job, because the people who do worst in a long outage are the ones with nothing to do and no information. The official advice supports the general shape of this rather than the particulars ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)).
{{/if}}

{{#if scenario:pandemic}}
**Isolation and bereavement together are the hardest part of an outbreak.** Speak to the person in the sickroom through the door by name every hour or two, so that the room is not silent. Name a second carer before the first is ill, and make the carer eat, sleep and be relieved, because exhaustion is what ends good nursing. Grief without a funeral needs something in its place: a letter read aloud, a candle, a date written down for a proper service later ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief)). Fear of the illness is information about what someone needs, not weakness. None of this paragraph is published guidance; it is what nursing at home actually asks of a household.
{{/if}}

{{#if scenario:economic-collapse}}
**Money trouble carries shame, and shame is what stops people asking.** Ask directly whether someone has thought of harming themselves, remove the means, and do not leave that person alone ([Samaritans](kiwix:wikipedia_en_all_maxi/Samaritans_(charity))). Put the household's money on one sheet of paper, what comes in, what must be paid and what can wait, because a figure written down frightens less than one imagined. Alcohol makes every part of it worse. Anyone drinking heavily every day must not stop suddenly and must not manage it alone: abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium tremens, so get a GP or alcohol service involved, and if it has already started with shaking, sweating, confusion or a fit — [[call 999]] ([Chronic conditions](page:chronic-conditions)).
{{/if}}

{{#if scenario:long-rebuild}}
**Over months and years, routine is the treatment.** Restart the ordinary things early: some kind of school for the children, work with a beginning and an end for the adults, a day off, a meal eaten together, something that marks the week. Mark the anniversaries and the deaths rather than passing over them ([Death and grief](page:death-and-grief)). Watch both for the person who has stopped washing, eating or speaking and for the one holding everyone else together; each of them needs somebody to sit with them. That much is experience rather than guidance, but the trauma advice points the same way ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/)).
{{/if}}

## UK specifics

- Grief, and the practical steps around a death, from confirming it to registering it, are on [Death and grief](page:death-and-grief). Most grief eases to a manageable level within weeks to a few months; grief that is still severe and daily after six months needs help ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 395](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=395); [Grief](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Grief)).
- NHS mental health services are reached through 111 (option 2 in England), and NHS Talking Therapies in England takes self-referrals without a GP ([Prepare, coping with trauma](kiwix:prepare_uk/prepare.campaign.gov.uk/coping-with-trauma/); [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)). The same practical approach applies in all four nations even where the local service differs.
- Anyone dependent on alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines or antidepressants whose supply is about to run out needs a taper, not a stop; the rules are on [Chronic conditions](page:chronic-conditions).
