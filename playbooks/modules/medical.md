## Key facts

- {{#if phones}}For anything life-threatening, [[call 999]]; for urgent advice in England, Scotland and Wales, [[call 111]] (Northern Ireland uses GP out-of-hours numbers instead); and use Pharmacy First in England for seven minor conditions; the full list is on [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers).{{else}}With the phones down, 999 and 111 are out of reach: treat what you can from the quick cards, send a runner to the nearest fire, ambulance or police station for anything life-threatening, and ask a pharmacy in person for the rest ([getting help without phones](page:no-phones); [UK numbers](page:uk-numbers)).{{/if}}
- Keep two weeks of any prescribed medicine, and ask the GP about "sick day rules" for insulin, steroids and heart drugs before an emergency, not during one. Antibiotics: never buy from unregulated sellers (fakes, resistance); keep any prescribed course you hold; in a total collapse, expired antibiotics keep most of their potency and are used under the rules in Where There Is No Doctor ([WTIND, p. 381](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=381); [Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 215](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=215)).
- Chronic conditions are the quiet killer of a long emergency. Very few of them are immediately dangerous when treatment stops, but insulin-dependent diabetes and lung disease that needs oxygen are, and steroids, epilepsy medicines, thyroid tablets, anticoagulants, dialysis and daily drink or drugs all have rules for when the supply runs short ([Survival and Austere Medicine, p. 105](doc:survival-austere-medicine-2017#page=105)). They are on [Chronic conditions](page:chronic-conditions): how to keep insulin without a fridge, why nobody stops steroids or epilepsy tablets abruptly, what to do when a dialysis unit or oxygen concentrator loses power, and which medicines keep.
- Sepsis can follow any infection and kills quickly; know the warning signs and treat them as an emergency — [[call 999]] ([NHS sepsis](kiwix:nhs_uk/www.nhs.uk/conditions/sepsis/); [Sepsis card](card:sepsis)).

## What to do

1. Stock a home kit with UK names and adult doses: paracetamol 500 mg tablets, up to 1 g four times a day and never more than 4 g in 24 hours ([NHS paracetamol](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/paracetamol-for-adults/)); ibuprofen 400 mg up to three times a day with food, avoided with asthma, kidney or stomach problems ([NHS ibuprofen](kiwix:nhs_medicines/www.nhs.uk/medicines/ibuprofen-for-adults/)); aspirin 300 mg to chew during a suspected heart attack ([Heart attack card](card:heart-attack)).
2. Add antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, chlorphenamine), loperamide, oral rehydration salts, chlorhexidine antiseptic, dressings, a thermometer, a pulse oximeter, gloves and a tick tool.
3. Wounds: stop the bleeding first ([severe bleeding](card:severe-bleeding)), then clean, dress and watch the wound as the [wound cleaning card](card:wound-cleaning) describes, and close only a clean, fresh cut ([wound closure](card:wound-closure)). Bites and stings, including adder bites, are on the [bites and stings card](card:bites-stings), with the background on [Ticks and adders](page:ticks-adders).
4. Tetanus when nobody can advise: a deep, dirty, puncture or bite wound in someone whose vaccinations are not complete or not known gets thorough washing with boiled water and soap, is never stitched closed, and, if you hold them, a penicillin-class antibiotic started at once; a stiff jaw or trouble swallowing in the days after is tetanus, and half of those who get it die even in hospital, so prevention is everything ([WTIND, p. 138](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=138); [WTIND, p. 233](doc:where-there-is-no-doctor#page=233)).
5. If a source in this library uses American terms, translate them: acetaminophen is paracetamol, epinephrine is adrenaline, albuterol is salbutamol.
6. In a mass-casualty situation, sort the injured before treating anyone, using the triage method in [Emergency War Surgery, p. 38](doc:emergency-war-surgery-2018#page=38).
7. Use the WHO essential medicines list as a shopping list for a community pharmacy or first-aid store ([WHO EML, p. 12](doc:who-eml-2025#page=12)).

## UK specifics

- The UK childhood schedule gives a full course of tetanus vaccine; an adult who has had fewer than three doses, or does not know, needs a booster after a puncture wound; US guidance is a booster every ten years ([Tetanus](kiwix:wikipedia_en_medicine_maxi/Tetanus)). A dirty wound in someone not fully vaccinated needs medical advice, not just a plaster.
- Ticks, Lyme disease and adders are covered fully on [Ticks and adders](page:ticks-adders).
- Confirming death follows [Ship Captain's Medical Guide ch. 12, p. 1](doc:scmg-ch12#page=1); care of the body, registering the death (five days in England and Wales, eight in Scotland), the coroner and temporary burial are on [Death and grief](page:death-and-grief).
- New quick cards this round cover head injury, spinal injury, poisoning, electric shock and lightning, fever in a child, sepsis, sprains and strains, frostbite, bites and stings, dental abscess, wound closure, eye injury, pregnancy emergencies and nosebleeds; they are listed below.
